Young Adults (19–30)

Race, Gender, Class — Think Like a Son, Not a Slave

Galatians 3:27–4:7 · Sunday 13 September 2026 · St Paul's Anglican Church Kogarah · Young Adults (19–30) · preached by Rev David Yung

Big Idea You are baptised into Christ, clothed with him, one in him. So don’t identify yourself as a slave would, but as God does: his adopted son.

Aim

Members can name Paul’s three markers and explain why union with Christ reorders identity at its root.

Members can say what “clothed with Christ” and “adopted as sons” mean, and why that is steadier ground than self-definition or the approval of a feed.

Each member names one person in their share house, their course, their workplace or their group chat whom they have filed under a category, and one step this week.

You'll need Bibles (CSB), pens, ~60–75 minutes

Connect (10 min)

  • Your Instagram bio, your LinkedIn headline, the way you introduce yourself in a new tutorial — pick one and read it out. How much work is that line doing, and who is it doing the work for?
  • Think about the last time your group chat decided what someone was. How quickly did the label stick, and how hard would it be for that person to get out from under it?

Read — Galatians 3:27–4:7 (CSB)

27 For those of you who were baptised into Christ have been clothed with Christ. 28 There is no Jew or Greek, slave or free, male and female; since you are all one in Christ Jesus. 29 And if you belong to Christ, then you are Abraham’s seed, heirs according to the promise.

4:1 Now I say that as long as the heir is a child, he differs in no way from a slave, though he is the owner of everything. 2 Instead, he is under guardians and trustees until the time set by his father. 3 In the same way we also, when we were children, were in slavery under the elements of the world.

4 When the time came to completion, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law, 5 to redeem those under the law, so that we might receive adoption as sons. 6 And because you are sons, God sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, “Abba, Father!” 7 So you are no longer a slave but a son, and if a son, then God has made you an heir.

Context

Paul writes to churches where Jewish and Greek Christians shared a room and were splitting over who really belonged. Three markers sorted the ancient world: race, class and gender. A Jewish man’s morning blessing thanked God he wasn’t a Gentile, a peasant or a woman — and nobody thought that strange. Our version is subtler and no less real. Paul’s move is not to pretend the categories don’t exist but to dethrone them: everyone baptised into Christ is clothed with Christ, and that is now the identity that outranks the rest.

Discuss

  1. What does Paul say has happened to those baptised into Christ? Why clothing, of all images — and what does it say that the clothes are handed to you rather than chosen?
  2. Race, class, gender. We’d never say the Jewish morning blessing out loud. So where does the same instinct show up in us — in who we assume is worth listening to, or in how we talk about the other gender?
  3. “All one in Christ Jesus.” You are still whatever you are — a student, a graduate, Chinese-Australian, male or female. So what has actually changed, and what hasn’t?
  4. Heirs according to the promise. If your identity rests on what God has promised rather than on what you have achieved so far, what pressure does that take off a twenty-four-year-old?
  5. An heir who owns everything and still lives like a slave. What are “the elements of the world” in your world — the unwritten rules of your course, your industry, your feed — and how do they function like a master?
  6. The law restrains but does not transform. Where are you relying on a rule, a routine or an app to hold you in check where only Christ can actually change you?
  7. Trace the Father, the Son and the Spirit through verses 4–6. Why does it matter that God did not send advice but sent his Son, and then the Spirit of his Son into our hearts?
  8. “No longer a slave but a son.” Where are you still living as a slave — performing for a category, or quietly filing someone else under one? What does thinking like a son look like in your house, your work, your chat this week?

Live It Out

  • Pray for 3 — three people from your course, your job or your house who aren’t Christians yet. Names written down. Prayed for daily.
  • Connect with 2 — two of them get real time this week, not a message. Coffee, a lift, a meal at your place.
  • Invite 1 — ask one of them to something at St Paul’s, or to the next young adults gathering. You don’t have to pre-qualify anybody.
  • 0 left unloved — and inside the church: KOGKids, KOGMini and KOGYouth need six more qualified assistants this quarter, and the Chinese Parallel congregation needs four accompanists. If you can play, or can hold a room of Year 7s, that is your name on this list.

This week's challenge: This week, have one unhurried conversation — phone down — with someone you’ve quietly categorised: the housemate you find hard, the person in your tute you’ve written off, the older member at church you assume has nothing in common with you. Ask a real question. Listen to the whole answer.

Memory verse: “So you are no longer a slave but a son, and if a son, then God has made you an heir.” — Galatians 4:7 (CSB)

Pray

  • Thank God that your identity was settled by his Son and not by your results, your job title, or the reception a post gets.
  • Pray by name for your three — that God’s kindness would lead them to repentance, and for one clear opportunity to point one of them to Jesus.
  • Ask God to make St Paul’s one church on his mission — not sorted by language, age or stage — and pray for those stepping up to serve KOGKids, KOGMini, KOGYouth and the Chinese Parallel congregation.

Leader's Notes

Joyfully point to Jesus, and assume there are people in the room still working out what they believe — this passage is good news for them, because identity here is received rather than constructed. Questions 2 and 8 can get raw quickly, especially around gender and call-out culture; go first with your own example, let people pass, and don’t let the group turn into a trial of someone who isn’t there. Keep bringing it back to the sermon’s move: before we point the finger, we take a good look at ourselves.

Question helps

  • Q1 — Baptised into Christ, clothed with Christ. Clothing, because it comes from outside you: the beggar invited in and given new clothes, the stranger brought into a wedding and handed wedding clothes. Compare Galatians 2:20.
  • Q2 — Don’t let this stay abstract. The sermon names it plainly: thanking God for our nation or race rather than a poorer one; dismissing someone’s job while being quietly glad about our own pay; women dismissing men as toxic brutes and men dismissing women as objects to pursue. We have not advanced from the ancient world as far as we think.
  • Q3 — The ranking has changed, not the facts. We are still all those things; they are simply no longer the most important markers. Christ is.
  • Q4 — It relocates the whole question. Our identity rests not on what we have now but on what God has done for us in Christ and promised us in the future — exactly what a season of constant change needs.
  • Q5 — The heir owns everything on paper but lives under guardians and trustees until the father’s appointed time. “The elements of the world” are a culture’s elementary teachings — its social rules and common sense — and they work like slave masters.
  • Q6 — For Israel it was God’s own Scripture and law that imprisoned and guarded them: not because the law was bad but because we are. It restrains rebellion for a time, but restraint is not transformation. It condemns justly and offers no hope of freedom.
  • Q7 — The Father sends the Son, born of a woman and born under the law, who lives the righteous life the law cannot convict and then takes its conviction to redeem us; the Spirit of the Son is sent into our hearts crying “Abba, Father”. All three persons working so that we can actually speak to the Father. Luther noticed Paul sets the Hebrew “Abba” beside the Greek “Pater” so everyone heard it in their heart language.
  • Q8 — Push for specifics and a date. The sermon’s application: when someone new visits, don’t identify them merely by their accent or their job, and don’t ignore them because you don’t speak their language. Think instead as someone clothed in Jesus — how can I help this person walk toward Jesus, not away from him?
