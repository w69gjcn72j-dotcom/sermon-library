When was the last time you physically walked into a bank?

I don’t go to banks that often, but I remember one time, I had to physically walk into a bank to get some banking done.

While I was waiting, I couldn’t help but overhear an old couple speaking with the bank manager.

The old gentleman said, “But I didn’t know. I thought he was really from the bank.”

The bank manager said, “Yes I know, but since you pressed the button, there’s little we could do. However, come into the office and we’ll see what we can do.”

Sadly, it sounded like the old couple was scammed by someone who pretended to be from the bank and lost a substantial amount of money.

They were persuaded by the scammer and lost their money.

I felt sorry for them.

They are one of many people in Australia who had lost money to scammers, people who pretended to be somebody else.

In fact, in 2022, Australians lost over $10 million were lost to scammers.

Would you be able to tell whether the person speaking to you was a real banker or a scammer?

But even more crucial than telling the difference between a scammer and a banker is the ability to tell the difference between whether something or someone is from God, or not.

Would you be able to tell whether the person telling you about who God is, is telling the truth, or whether he or she is a scammer?

How do we determine whether someone is telling a message from God or not?

We are going to see that it’s not always obvious to tell whether someone’s from God or not.

Counsel from a respected unbeliever

Last week, we read that different people responded differently to the good news of Jesus.

There were those who didn’t dare to follow Jesus, there were those who dared to believe in Jesus, and there were those who were jealous of Jesus’ followers.

Those who were jealous of following Jesus were the High Priest, along with a group called the Sadducees.

The Sadducees were Jews who didn’t believe in the resurrection or in angels.

They probably made up most of the Sanhedrin, the council that put Peter and other apostles in jail out of jealousy.

When Peter and the apostles replied that they must obey God rather than man, and that God had exalted Jesus to be the Ruler and Saviour, the High Priest, along with the Sadducees in the Sanhedrin got very angry; they were enraged!

They were so enraged that they were ready to kill Peter and the apostles.

Their jealousy was about to turn them into murderers!

However, one of those in the Sanhedrin wasn’t a Sadducee; he was a Pharisee.

Unlike the Sadducees, the Pharisees believed in the general resurrection (but not the resurrection of Jesus) and they believe in the existence of angels.

This Pharisee was called Gamaliel.

Gamaliel was a respected teacher of the law; everyone respected Rabbi Gamaliel.

He was so respected that in the Mishnah, a collection of teachings in the first century, said that “since Rabban Gamaliel the elder died there has been no more reverence for the law; and purity and abstinence died out at the same time.”

Gamaliel had a major influence on how people thought about the law of God.

Open up with me to Acts chapter 5 verse 34 to 35 to see what Gamaliel did when the Sanhedrin was about to kill Peter and the apostles,

“But a Pharisee named Gamaliel, a teacher of the law who was respected by all the people, stood up in the Sanhedrin and ordered the men to be taken outside for a little while. He said to them, “Men of Israel, be careful about what you’re about to do to these men.”

Gamaliel de-escalated the rising temperature against the Christians in the room.

Have you heard of the term, de-escalation?

That’s when a person or a group getting increasingly upset about something and you bring down the temperature, you de-escalate the situation by bringing calmness and understanding to the situation.

If you had ever taught children, whether your own or not, you know that there are times when you need to call time-outs.

If my home, when my children get naughty, which is actually not that often now days, (in fact, it hardly happens at all), but when they do, we would tell them to go to the naughty corner.

We needed to de-escalate the situation and for the children to calm themselves.

Wouldn’t it be good if we can tell adults to do the same?

Well, that’s what Gamaliel did; he separated the angry Sanhedrin from the apostles.

He told the Sanhedrin to think about what they’re doing.

He reminded them about what happened to Theudas, who claimed to be somebody.

He rallied 400 people to him and was eventually killed.

In the original text, the word translated as his followers in English (附從他 in Chinese), can be translated as those persuaded by him.

All those who were persuaded by him eventually scattered.

The same thing happened to someone called Judas (not the Judas who betrayed Jesus).

He also attracted a crowd but when after he died, those who were persuaded by him were also scattered.

What is Gamliel’s implication? Verses 38-39, “So in the present case, I tell you, stay away from these men and leave them alone. For if this plan or this work is of human origin, it will fail; 39 but if it is of God, you will not be able to overthrow them. You may even be found fighting against God.” They were persuaded by him.”

Stay away from these men! Time will tell the true origin of these men’s message.

If the message is from men, they will perish like Therudas and Judas.

If the message is from God, the Sanhedrin will find themselves fighting against God.

And the Sanhedrin was…persuaded!

It made sense.

I don’t know about you, but I find it very persuasive; it does make so much sense.

And Gamaliel’s de-escalation exercise seemed to have worked.

The Sanhedrin didn’t kill Peter and the apostles.

They got flogged, which must have been very painful, but at least they were still alive

But even as I was preparing this sermon, one word stood out to me: The Sanhedrin was persuaded.

The word ‘persuade’ is not a bad word.

In the original language, it’s used both positively and negatively.

In Matthew chapter 27 verse 43, the word is used to describe Jesus’ trust in God

Those at the foot of the cross of Jesus were saying, “He trusts in God (literally, he is persuaded by God); let God rescue him now—if he takes pleasure in him! For he said, ‘I am the Son of God.’”

However, in the context of this passage, the word persuaded is used three times and two of them were used negatively of persuasive men leading others astray.

When see this word for the third time, when Gamaliel persuades the Sanhedrin to let the apostles go,

Was it used positively or negatively?

Was it a good advice or a bad advice?

How do we deal with advice from Gamaliel, who doesn’t believe in Jesus, about how we go about understanding God’s will?

I remember that as I was growing up in my old church, there were Mormons in our suburbs who would spend a lot of time speaking to the public, every day.

By the way, Mormons don’t believe in the God that we believe in; they don’t believe in God the Father, God the Son and God the Holy Spirit.

Their belief is not the same as what the Bible teaches.

These Mormons were trying to persuade people on the streets to follow them.

They worked hard!

Some might call them sincere, even pious.

And I still remember someone from Church saying that we should learn from them.

Of course, as a kid, I was shocked.

Why would we want to learn from the Mormons?

The reply was that we’re not to believe them but to learn from their diligence.

Don’t believe them; just learn their techniques, learn how they do their mission.

But can we just learn from their techniques?

Can we learn more about how to serve God from them, even though we serve a different God?

And generally, what should we do with the advice that unbelievers give us about following God?

The problem of defining God’s work

The problem with unbelievers’ advice about following God is that it’s often not very good advice.

Though they appear to be wise, if you go a little deeper, you’ll see that they are not very wise counsel at all.

Take the example of Gamaliel’s advice.

It’s true that he de-escalated the situation, but he didn’t ask the most fundamental question: How do you define whether someone is from God?

How do you know whether the message someone is giving is from God or not?

This definition of how we determine whether someone is from God or not is crucial to the way we respond to various situations in life.

Take for example, the medal rally count in the Olympics.

How do you determine whether a nation is the most successful sporting nation in the world?

According to the international Olympic committee, the more gold medals you have, the more successful you are as a sporting nation.

However, some nations (eg USA) use the total number of medals won to determine how successful you are as a nation.

Since they had a lot more silver and bronze medals than gold medals at one stage, they put themselves on the top of the leaderboard.

There are other nations which count the number of medals and divided it by the population of the country.

If that’s the case, the most successful nation would be New Zealand, which had one medal for every 427,000 people.

This is followed by Australia, which had one medal for every 604,651 people.

Your definition of what success looks like will determine which nation you consider to be the most successful.

Similarly, your definition of what God’s approval look like will determine whether you consider someone’s message is from God or not.

Most of the times, our world, as well as many Christians, define God’s approval (or God’s blessings) to be the good things of life.

If you have success at work, if you have health, if you’re well-off, if you have a lovely family, then you’re blessed! You’re approved by God!

However, if you have a tough life, facing failures after failures, struggling with loneliness, then you’re not blessed by God. You might even be cursed by God!

That’s how Gamaliel determines whether the Good news of Jesus, the message that Peter and the apostles preach, is from God or not.

This is Gamaliel’s formula, as it were: Just wait and see.

If the movement lasts, it’s from God.

If the movement doesn’t last, it’s not from God.

However, this is a dangerous formula, for it judges whether a person is from God according to man’s standard, according to what man considers as glory.

Gamaliel’s formula is neither good nor wise; it’s bad and foolish.

Let’s take a Church that I visited during my leave very briefly during my leave.

It’s a very big and historical church.

There were so many people in one service that there were people looking into the service from the outside.

Ok, maybe they were just tourists like me who weren’t going in anyway, but the inside of the Church was pretty full.

Just outside the Church is a bookstore.

It sold many Christian books and Christian souvenirs.

However, as I was looking through them, I found a postcard with a big Buddha on it.

I know that it’s a local tourist spot, but to have it sold in a Church, really?

Contrast this Church with a church plant by a minister called Jai.

Jai started a Church in Mackay in Queensland, and he hoped to increase it to 150 within three years.

However, as the three year mark approached, they only had 30 people coming regularly.

Should he keep going or should he just give up?

If we use Gamaliel’s formula, if it lasts, it’s from God but if it doesn’t it’s from man, then our conclusion would be this:

God blesses the big Church, even though they may be selling Buddha postcards,

God doesn’t approve Jai’s Church, even though he taught the Bible well.

However, this is a very reductionistic approach.

It reduces God to a formula; it wants to make God predictable.

But God is not predictable.

Who would’ve predicted that God would send his own Son, the Lord Jesus Christ, to die on a cross?

Who would’ve predicted that the disciples of Jesus would scatter?

If we applied Gamaliel’s formula straight after Jesus’ death on the cross, we would say that Jesus was just like the other false teachers.

Their effort came to nothing so it’s only a movement started by man.

But Jesus was indeed from God.

Jesus rose from the dead on the third day, according to the Scriptures.

The fulfilment of the Old Testament prophecies as well as the signs and wonders done by Jesus himself and the apostles after Jesus was taken up to heaven, show that Jesus is indeed from God.

God used sinners, even the murderers of his Son, to achieve the greatest act of salvation of all time.

The way we determine God’s blessings, God’s approval, mustn’t be based on man’s understanding of glory.

Gamaliel’s formula can’t be trusted because it’s faulty.

God will move in ways that are far beyond our understanding; his ways cannot be predicted according to our own understanding.

He sets the rules of success, not us.

And so, we must trust him, not ourselves.

The great reformation theologian described people who see man’s success, man’s glory, as a measure of God’s approval, as theologians of glory.

He said that a theologian of glory is one “who looks upon the invisible things of God as though they were clearly perceptible in those things which have actually happened."

A theologian of glory see human success, whether it’s in the strength of people or the achievements of organisations, as indicators of God’s approval.

They define success based on appearance.

It might seem wise at first but it’s folly, for they try to define God’s work by man’s standard, by what we consider as glorious.

Don’t be fooled by appearances; don’t fall into the trap of the theology of glory.

The joy of shamed believers

Instead, be captured by the theology of the cross.

In contrast to the theologians of glory, Luther said that a theologian of the Cross "comprehends the visible and manifest things of God seen through suffering and the Cross."

And that’s exactly what Peter and the apostles did.

Look with me at Acts chapter 5 verse 41, “Then they went out from the presence of the Sanhedrin, rejoicing that they were counted worthy to be treated shamefully on behalf of the Name.”

They were flogged but they were rejoicing!

They rejoiced not because they enjoyed the pain of being flogged.

They rejoiced because they were counted worthy to suffer on behalf of Christ’s name.

Referring to this passage, the Christian Theologian Don Carson said, “the apostles are (dare I say it?) relieved.”

They were relieved to suffer for Christ’s name!

Imagine a family going to a bowling alley.

There are Dads, Granddads, Mums and kids.

One of the boys got a bowling ball and was about to throw it down the alley.

But before he starts to bowl, the gutter of his alley is suddenly guarded by rails or barriers.

These are there so that beginners like the little boy won’t feel bad when they bowl badly and the ball goes down the gutter.

Has anyone use the rail when they bowled?

Well, one of the grandfathers saw it and told the boy to not use the rails.

The rest of the family was shocked, but the grandfather said the boy had to learn to face failures.

The family complied and the boy started bowling.

Sure enough, the ball kept going down the gutter.

In fact, the bowling ball went down the gutter every time he bowled.

The boy was frustrated, crying, threw a tantrum on the floor, and everyone in the bowling centre was looking at his score.

He was about to score a perfect 0.

When you bowl in a bowling alley, you usually have 20 chances.

He used up his 19; he had one more left.

With all eyes on him, he bowled one last time.

The bowling ball started rolling, closer and closer to the gutter, until the very end, when he hit one pin.

As soon as he hit the last pin, the whole bowling centre erupted with joy.

The boy was so happy he finally got one and he thanked his grandfather for it.

The rest of the family tried to protect him from failure by putting up the rail; but the boy’s grandfather believed that the boy was ready.

He knew that the boy would feel horrible at the time, but he knew that the boy was now ready.

The first Christians were so happy because God considered them ready to take on the suffering and shame that comes with being a follower of Jesus.

God counted worthy to be flogged, to be treated shamefully in the eyes of the world, just as Jesus himself was treated shamefully by the world.

They considered it an honour to suffer shamefully for Christ’s name.

We see this theme of suffering for Christ again and again in the Bible.

Jesus himself said in Matthew chapter five verses 11-12, ““You are blessed when they insult you and persecute you and falsely say every kind of evil against you because of me. 12 Be glad and rejoice, because your reward is great in heaven. For that is how they persecuted the prophets who were before you.”

When you suffer for Jesus Christ, you’re blessed, you’re counted in the same company as the prophets and the apostles!

James, Jesus’ brother, said in James chapter 1 verse 2, “Consider it a great joy, my brothers and sisters, whenever you experience various trials.

And Paul tells Timothy in 2 Timothy chapter 3:12, “In fact, all who want to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted.

It’s not if you will be persecuted, but when you will be persecuted.

If you want to live a godly life you will be persecuted.

The theology of glory is focusses on what man considers as glory.

The theology of the cross on the suffering that Jesus went through on the cross.

Just as Christ suffered, his followers will also suffer.

For a genuine follower of Jesus, suffering for Jesus is a badge of honour, even when we’re shamed by the world.

Though the Sanhedrin flogged the apostles to stop them from talking about Jesus, the apostles couldn’t stop talking about Jesus!

They kept talking about Jesus not only in the temple but also in homes.

Their suffering only made them more joyful to tell others about Jesus.

Do you want to have joy as a Christian?

If you do, then don’t ask God to take away the shame and suffering that comes from being a Christian.

Instead ask for the faith to boldly suffer because of Jesus.

Hold on to Jesus even when most of the world, most of our friends and most of our families don’t believe in him.

Holding on to Jesus don’t mean just hanging in there but to go out.

Go out to teach the children in the Children Centre and the local schools.

Go out to other countries to tell people about Jesus.

Ezekiel will tell us about his experience of telling people in Fiji later in the service.

Go out and be prepared to leave your comfort behind; find the joy that comes from knowing that God considers you worthy to suffer for the sake of His Son.

Hugh Latimer was the Bishop of Worcester in England in the 1500’s.

He was from a family of farmers but eventually became a well-known preacher.

He preached that Jesus was the only way to God the Father, and not according to what the Roman Cathholics were teaching at that time.

For that, he was sentence to be burnt at the stake.

A few years ago, our family went to the very site where he and his friend Ridley was burnt.

On the very place where he was burnt, there was a cross.

As they were being prepared to be burnt, Latimer said this to his friend Ridely, “Be of good comfort, Master Ridley, and play the man.  We shall this day light such a candle by God's grace in England as I trust shall never be put out."

According to the theology of glory, according to Gamliel’s formula, and the Roman Catholic Church, neither Latimer nor Ridley were approved by God.

But according to the theology of the cross, both Latimer and Ridley can be comforted in the joy of knowing that God had found them worthy to be burnt like a candle for Christ’s glory.

Will God find us worthy to suffer for his Son?

Will we find this joy that comes from knowing that God considers us worthy?

If you are not a Christian yet, then please, don’t use Gamliel’s formula, don’t use man’s glory to judge whether something is from God.

Instead, consider the cross of Jesus Christ.

In the cross of Jesus Christ, God has shown his matchless love for unworthy people like us.

In the resurrection of Jesus Christ, he has shown his life-giving power.

If you want to find out more about what the cross of Jesus Christ means, then please come and see me, Range, En Bo or Mark or Vera to find out more.

If you are a Christian, then look for joy in suffering for Christ.

Only those whom God finds worthy, those who have accepted and believed in Christ, would God allow to suffer for Christ.

Only those whom God considers worthy get to suffer for Christ.

However, if you are not willing to suffer, if you have never suffered for Christ, then you won’t know the joy and assurance that God wants you to have.

If you want comfort rather than joy, then you are not worthy in God’s eyes to suffer for Christ’s name.

Friends, fools define God’s work by man’s glory; Christians rejoice when they’re shamed for Jesus, for God’s counted them worthy.

Gamliel taught many students, the most famous student being Saul of Tarsus.

Saul hated the Christians.

Unlike Gamaliel, Saul thought that it was far too dangerous to have a wait and see approach to find out whether someone was misrepresenting God.

Saul was actively persecuting the Christians.

And yet God had mercy on him.

Jesus saved him and considered him worthy to suffer for his sake.

Gamaliel wrongly defined God’s work according to man’s glory.

However, God counted Gamaliel’s student, Saul, renamed Paul, worthy to suffer for Christ’s sake.

Paul became a theologian of the cross, whereas Gamaliel remained a theologian of glory.

Are you joyful? Are you happy to be a Christian?

I often hear Christians, myself included, complain about how bad things are, how hard life is, how little we have.

But the Bible tells Christians to be joyful, not only in the good times but also in the hard times, even in times when we’re shamefully treated by the world.

If you want to feel rejected, try handing out Christian brochures to people on the street.

Or try inviting people to Church only to be met with rejection after rejection.

Of course, these are only small rejections compared with the suffering the many other brothers and sisters go through.

But are we ready to even suffer a little for Jesus?

When we face suffering for Christ, whether it’s big or small, may we count it all joy.

For God has counted us ready, worthy to suffer for Christ.

Here are the three questions for this week:

How do you define a successful Church?

What trap can we fall into when we define what a successful Church is?

Have you ever been shamed for being a Christian and rejoiced?

Fools define God’s work by man’s glory; Christians rejoice when they’re shamed for Jesus, for God’s counted them.

Let’s pray, “Father, thank you for Jesus, whom you sent to die on the cross for our sins. In the cross of Christ, we see your unmatched love for us. On the cross, we see your glory. Forgive us for the times when we were persuaded by man’s glory rather than the glory of the cross. We ask you not to take our suffering for Christ away from us. Instead give us the faith and the boldness to suffer the same of the cross, that we might rejoice that you have counted us worthy. By your Spirit, make us worthy to suffer for Jesus, in whose name we pray, Amen.”
