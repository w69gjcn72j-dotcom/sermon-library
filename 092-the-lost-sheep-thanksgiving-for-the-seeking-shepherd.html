The Lost Sheep — Thanksgiving for the Seeking Shepherd

Luke 15:1-7 · guest sermon, 2024
Big IdeaThere is rejoicing in heaven over one sinner who repents, for the Shepherd seeks and finds his lost sheep.

Luke 15:1-7 – St Paul’s Kogarah – 11 Nov 2024

It is a pleasure the be with you today to commence a three week series on Thanksgiving.

Our Bible passage today reminds us that we have much to thank God for.

Our reading from Luke 15:1-7 is the first of three parables that Jesus told about ‘lost’ things – a lost sheep, a lost coin, and a lost son (sometimes known as ‘the prodigal son’).

As we look at the first parable, it is important to recognise the context for all three parables, to whom he was speaking, and why.

The context is in verse 1 - “the tax collectors and ‘sinners’ were all gathering around to hear him.’

But Jesus is not primarily speaking to these people, but to another group - ‘the Pharisees and the teachers of the law’.

Verse 3 tells us that it was to these people in particular that Jesus spoke these three parables.

He did this because they were muttering ‘This man welcomes sinners and eats with them’”. (v2)

The religious leaders of Jesus’ day are complaining because of the people Jesus is associated with.

They regarded these people as “sinners” - people who have broken God’s laws in extreme ways.

The tax-collector in the first century was not merely a federal government employee doing a job

He was a collaborator with the enemy, who generated great wealth by extorting his own countrymen and pocketing an excessive margin on the tax.

The other “sinners” probably includes people like the woman in Luke 7, who had committed some kind of sexual immorality.

Before we too quickly write-off the Pharisees and the teachers of the law for their hypocrisy, imagine how you would react if Jesus had come to 21st century Australia and spent his time with tax cheats and terrorists.

I suspect that we would be shocked and perhaps even outraged – just as they were.

This is not the first time that Jesus has met with tax collectors and ‘sinners’, and it is not the first time that the religious leaders have had a go at Jesus for doing so.

Back in Luke 5, the Pharisees had complained to the disciples of Jesus that their master ate and drank with sinners.

Jesus said to them. 31 … “It is not the healthy who need a doctor, but the sick. 32 I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance.” (Luke 5:31-32)

All three parables in Luke 15 make a similar point – there is great joy when a lost thing is found.

Today’s parable is about a lost sheep. Jesus asks the Pharisees to put themselves in the sandals of a shepherd with 100 sheep, and one of the sheep goes missing.

A good shepherd doesn’t write off a single lost sheep as an acceptable stock shrinkage, to be accepted indifferently.

Jesus puts it to them - Surely any of them would leave the ninety-nine sheep in the open country and go after the lost sheep until he finds it.

And note especially the reaction to finding the lost sheep – great rejoicing that the lost has been found.

Jesus gives the application of the parable in verse 7.

7 I tell you that in the same way there will be more rejoicing in heaven over one sinner who repents than over ninety-nine righteous persons who do not need to repent.

God is like the shepherd in this parable. The lost sheep is the tax-collector or the prostitute who has gone astray.

God longs for the lost “sinner” to be found, and when they are found, there is great rejoicing in heaven.

Imagine that you are one of those Pharisee hearing this parable. What do you think Jesus is saying to you?

I suspect that they saw themselves as one of the “ninety-nine righteous persons who do not need to repent.”

And that Jesus was saying to them that they should be rejoicing that last sinners were being found, rather than complaining about it.

We aren’t going to look at the other two parables in any detail, but it is important to notice the numerical progression.

The first parable is about one lost sheep out of 100. The second parable is about 1 lost coin out of 10.

The third parable is one prodigal son out of two sons, though as it turns out, the two sons in the last parable are both lost, because both were estranged from their father.

By the end of the parable, the prodigal son has come to his senses, repented, and has been accepted back into family.

But the other brother, tragically, in still outside in the cold, unwilling to accept the father’s invitation to celebrate with the family.

This son doesn’t understand what it means to be a son – he thinks that it is all about being a slave and obeying orders.

He doesn’t realise that he is loved and accepted for who he is – he’s lived his life thinking he has to earn his father’s approval.

The prodigal son knows deserves to be treated like a slave, but the Father takes him back as a son.

This other son makes himself into a slave, because he has not understood what it is to be a part of the family. Both sons are lost.

In the three parables, the ratio of ‘lost’ to ‘righteous’ progressively shortens. One to ninety nine becomes one to nine, which becomes 100% lost in the third parable.

When the odds are 1 to 99, it is easy to be complacent – other people are the “sinners” who need Jesus; other people are the lost ones, not me.

The progression of the parable is confronting, as the odds shorten against us.

The message of the three parables is not merely “you should be happy when lost sinners are saved by Jesus”. The message is “we are all lost sinners who need to be saved by Jesus”.

Let me quote again Jesus words in Luke 5 - 32 I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance.” (Luke 5:31-32).

Later in Luke’s Gospel, Jesus says that he “came to seek and to save the lost” (19:10).

Elsewhere, the Apostle Paul writes Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners.

If you are not a sinner, Jesus hasn’t come to seek after you.

You don’t need him to save you, if you are righteous.

Or, to put that the other way around, Jesus can only save us if we admit that we are one of the lost; that we are one of those sinners who need to repent.

The Pharisees in Luke 15 presumed themselves to be the righteous.

That is an easy self-assessment to make when you are only comparing yourself to tax collectors and prostitutes.

But that kind of comparison doesn’t make you righteousness; it only makes you self-righteousness.

The proper standard for comparison is God’s perfect righteousness. And when we compare ourselves to that standard, we all fall short of the mark.

Every single one of us is a lost sheep, a lost coin, a lost son or daughter. A lost thing that needs to be found; a lost sinner who needs to repent.

Jesus said that he had come to call sinners to repentance, and a Christian is someone who has responded to the call by repenting.

A Christian is someone who has turned from ignoring or even rejecting Jesus, and turned to submit to him as Lord in their lives.

When they repented, there was great rejoicing in heaven, because this is what God longs for – the lost to found, for the sinner to turn back to him.

And not only is there great joy in heaven, there is also great thanksgiving in the heart of the new Christian.

This ought to be the foundation response of every Christian – being overwhelmed with thankfulness to God because of his undeserved love towards us.

Thankful that Jesus came to seek and save a lost one like me, to call a sinner like me to repentance.

Thankful, that God has been merciful and not given me what my sins deserve.

Thankful, that God gave his one and only son to die in my place, so that my sins could be forgiven.

Thankful, that God has lavishly welcomed me back into his family, like the way that Father welcomed his prodigal son back.

The three parables in Luke 15 are about the joy in heaven when a sinner repents.

Jesus says to us all this morning what he said at the outset of his public ministry –"The time has come. The kingdom of God is near. Repent and believe the good news!" (Mark 1:15)

Jesus has not come only for other people. He came for you.

Don’t be like the Pharisees who grumbled because other people were being saved, and missed out on salvation themselves.

Come to him, and know the joy of sins forgiven, and let your heart be filled with thankfulness.
