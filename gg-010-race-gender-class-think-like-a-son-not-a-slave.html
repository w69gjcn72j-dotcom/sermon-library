Race, Gender, Class — Think Like a Son, Not a Slave

Galatians 3:27–4:7 · Sunday 13 September 2026 · St Paul's Anglican Church Kogarah · Adults · preached by Rev David Yung

Big Idea You are baptised into Christ, clothed with him, one in him. So don’t identify yourself as a slave would, but as God does: his adopted son.

Aim

Members can name the three markers Paul lists and explain why union with Christ reorders all three.

Members can articulate what it means to be clothed with Christ and adopted as sons, and why that settles the question of identity better than self-assessment or other people’s opinions.

Each member identifies one relationship — at work, in the street, at morning tea — where they have been identifying someone by category, and one concrete step this week.

You'll need Bibles (CSB), pens, ~60–75 minutes

Connect (10 min)

  • At a party the first two questions are almost always “Where are you from?” and “What do you do?” How do you answer the second one — and be honest about how you polish it. (The sermon’s examples: “Waste Removal Engineer” for a garbage collector; “Brand Warrior” for someone in marketing.) Why do we do that?
  • Think back to morning tea last Sunday. Who did you end up standing with, and why? Was it accent, age, children’s ages, or the kind of work you do?

Read — Galatians 3:27–4:7 (CSB)

27 For those of you who were baptised into Christ have been clothed with Christ. 28 There is no Jew or Greek, slave or free, male and female; since you are all one in Christ Jesus. 29 And if you belong to Christ, then you are Abraham’s seed, heirs according to the promise.

4:1 Now I say that as long as the heir is a child, he differs in no way from a slave, though he is the owner of everything. 2 Instead, he is under guardians and trustees until the time set by his father. 3 In the same way we also, when we were children, were in slavery under the elements of the world.

4 When the time came to completion, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law, 5 to redeem those under the law, so that we might receive adoption as sons. 6 And because you are sons, God sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, “Abba, Father!” 7 So you are no longer a slave but a son, and if a son, then God has made you an heir.

Context

Paul writes to churches where Jewish and Gentile Christians sat in the same room and were pulling apart over who really belonged. Three markers categorised the ancient world: race, class and gender. The Jewish morning cycle of blessings had a man thank God that he had not been made a Gentile, a boor or a woman — one group looking down on another was entirely normal. Paul’s answer is not to abolish the differences but to dethrone them: everyone baptised into Christ is clothed with Christ, and Christ is now the identity that outranks the rest.

Discuss

  1. What does Paul say has happened to those baptised into Christ? Why “clothed” rather than, say, “taught” or “improved”?
  2. Race, class, gender. Before we point the finger at the first-century Jew, where do we quietly thank God that we are not like someone else — our nation, our pay bracket, the other gender?
  3. “You are all one in Christ Jesus.” We are still Chinese or Anglo, still male or female, still a tradesman or a teacher. So what exactly has changed?
  4. What does it mean that we are “Abraham’s seed, heirs according to the promise”? What are we actually waiting for?
  5. How can an heir own everything and still live no differently from a slave? How do the unwritten rules of your workplace or your culture function like a slave master?
  6. Paul says the law was our guardian until Christ. If God’s own law restrains our rebellion but cannot transform us, what are you relying on to restrain you rather than change you?
  7. Trace what each person of the Trinity does in verses 4–6. Why does it matter that God did not simply send a message but sent his Son, and then the Spirit of his Son into our hearts?
  8. “No longer a slave but a son.” Name one place — the office, the street, the school gate, church morning tea — where you have been identifying someone by their category rather than asking, “how can I help this person walk toward Jesus?” What changes this week?

Live It Out

KOG 3·2·1·0 — pray for 3, connect with 2, invite 1, 0 left unloved.

  • Pray for 3 — three people in your street, your workplace or your family who aren’t Christians yet. Write their names down tonight and pray for them by name each day.
  • Connect with 2 — two of those three get real contact this week: a coffee, a phone call, a hand with something. Not a pitch; a relationship.
  • Invite 1 — ask one of them to something at St Paul’s. There is no vetting at the door; the invitation is not yours to ration.
  • 0 left unloved — next Sunday, deliberately stand with someone whose accent, age or job means you would normally drift past them.

This week's challenge: This week, have one conversation with someone you would normally identify by their category — the neighbour whose English is hard work, the colleague two pay grades away, the person at church you have never spoken to. Ask them a real question and listen to the whole answer.

Memory verse: “So you are no longer a slave but a son, and if a son, then God has made you an heir.” — Galatians 4:7 (CSB)

Pray

  • Thank God for sending his Son to redeem us and adopt us as sons, and for sending the Spirit of his Son so that we can cry “Abba, Father”.
  • Pray by name for your three — that God’s kindness would lead them to repentance, and that he would give you an opportunity to point one of them to Jesus.
  • Ask God to make St Paul’s one church on God’s mission: not Jew or Greek, slave or free, male or female, but all one in Christ — and pray for the three new mid-week fellowships and for those grieving among us.

Leader's Notes

Joyfully point to Jesus. Assume guests and not-yet-Christians are in the room — this passage is good news for them, since identity here is given rather than achieved. Question 2 will get uncomfortable; go first with your own example rather than cornering anyone, and steer stories about other people back to the first person: “before we point the finger at the Jew, we need to take a good look at ourselves.” Note too that there has been much grief in our church family — twelve funerals and deaths in four months — so the closing material on identity holding firm when everything changes may land very close to home. Leave room for it.

Question helps

  • Q1 — Baptised into Christ and clothed with Christ. Clothing, because the identity is given from outside: a beggar invited in and given new clothes, or the stranger brought into a wedding and handed wedding clothes. Compare Galatians 2:20.
  • Q2 — Expect honesty, not performance. The sermon names three: thanking God for our nation or race rather than a poorer one; dismissing another person’s job while being quietly glad we are better paid; women dismissing men as toxic brutes and men dismissing women as objects to pursue.
  • Q3 — The ranking. We do not stop being any of those things; they are no longer the most important markers. “I identify with you not on the basis of whether you speak Chinese or English, but on the basis that we are in Christ.”
  • Q4 — Everything God promised Abraham’s seed. Not only saved by God’s One and Only Son, but adopted as sons — our identity resting not on what we have now but on what God has done in Christ and promised for the future.
  • Q5 — The heir owns everything on paper but lives under guardians and trustees until the father’s appointed time. “The elements of the world” are a culture’s elementary teachings — its social rules and common sense — and they function like slave masters.
  • Q6 — For Israel it was God’s own Scripture and law that imprisoned and guarded them: not because the law was bad but because we are. It restrains our rebellion for a time, but restraint is not transformation. It condemns justly and offers no hope of freedom.
  • Q7 — The Father sends; the Son, born of a woman and born under the law, lives the righteous life the law cannot convict and then takes its conviction to redeem us; the Spirit of the Son is sent into our hearts crying “Abba, Father”. All three at work so that we can actually relate to the Father. Luther noticed Paul sets a Hebrew word beside a Greek one so everyone heard it in their heart language.
  • Q8 — Press for the specific and the doable. The sermon’s own application: when someone new visits, don’t identify them merely by their accent or their job, and don’t ignore them because you don’t speak their language — that is slave thinking.

Our church vision

This passage drives our “from faith to faith” vision — a church whose identity is Christ rather than accent or income, growing toward 75 households serving densely at St Paul’s (we are at 50), on the way to 500 by 2035. Home groups are the engine, which is why three new mid-week fellowships (two Chinese, one English) is a live 90-day goal: ask whether your group should be multiplying. And follow up the dads who came to “A Father’s Invitation”.
