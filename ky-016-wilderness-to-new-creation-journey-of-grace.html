Wilderness to New Creation: Journey of Grace

Luke 3:1–38 · Sunday 11 October 2026 (157th Anniversary Sunday) · St Paul’s Anglican Church Kogarah · For high-school students · Sermon by Rev Jerry Zhu

Big Idea: God’s word comes to people who will listen, not to the powerful. It turns us around to bear real fruit, and in Jesus — who stood with sinners and is the Father’s beloved Son — flawed people like us are welcomed into God’s family of grace.

Aim: by the end, students will

see that God speaks to people who listen, and plan a few minutes of “wilderness time” without their phone;

tell the difference between feeling sorry and actually turning around, and name one “fruit” to try this week;

rest in being loved by the Father because of Jesus — not because of marks, likes, sport or their family’s faith.

You’ll need: Bibles (CSB), pens, paper, about 60–75 minutes.

1. Connect (10 min)

  • Who are the “important people” everyone at school is paying attention to right now — streamers, sports stars, influencers? Imagine God’s message went to someone nobody had heard of instead. How would that feel?
  • If you turned your phone off for a whole evening, what would you notice? When did you last have real quiet?

2. Read: Luke 3:1–38 (CSB)

1 In the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius Caesar, while Pontius Pilate was governor of Judea, Herod was tetrarch of Galilee, his brother Philip tetrarch of the region of Iturea and Trachonitis, and Lysanias tetrarch of Abilene, 2 during the high priesthood of Annas and Caiaphas, God’s word came to John the son of Zechariah in the wilderness. 3 He went into all the vicinity of the Jordan, proclaiming a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins, 4 as it is written in the book of the words of the prophet Isaiah:

A voice of one crying out in the wilderness: Prepare the way for the Lord; make his paths straight! 5 Every valley will be filled, and every mountain and hill will be made low; the crooked will become straight, the rough ways smooth, 6 and everyone will see the salvation of God.

7 He then said to the crowds who came out to be baptized by him, “Brood of vipers! Who warned you to flee from the coming wrath? 8 Therefore produce fruit consistent with repentance. And don’t start saying to yourselves, ‘We have Abraham as our father,’ for I tell you that God is able to raise up children for Abraham from these stones. 9 The ax is already at the root of the trees. Therefore, every tree that doesn’t produce good fruit will be cut down and thrown into the fire.”

10 “What then should we do?” the crowds were asking him. 11 He replied to them, “The one who has two shirts must share with someone who has none, and the one who has food must do the same.” 12 Tax collectors also came to be baptized, and they asked him, “Teacher, what should we do?” 13 He told them, “Don’t collect any more than what you have been authorized.” 14 Some soldiers also questioned him, “What should we do?” He said to them, “Don’t take money from anyone by force or false accusation, and be satisfied with your wages.”

15 Now the people were waiting expectantly, and all of them were questioning in their hearts whether John might be the Messiah. 16 John answered them all, “I baptize you with water, but one who is more powerful than I am is coming. I am not worthy to untie the strap of his sandals. He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire. 17 His winnowing shovel is in his hand to clear his threshing floor and gather the wheat into his barn, but the chaff he will burn with fire that never goes out.” 18 Then, along with many other exhortations, he proclaimed good news to the people. 19 But when John rebuked Herod the tetrarch because of Herodias, his brother’s wife, and all the evil things he had done, 20 Herod added this to everything else — he locked up John in prison.

21 When all the people were baptized, Jesus also was baptized. As he was praying, heaven opened, 22 and the Holy Spirit descended on him in a physical appearance like a dove. And a voice came from heaven: “You are my beloved Son; with you I am well-pleased.”

23 As he began his ministry, Jesus was about thirty years old and was thought to be the son of Joseph, son of Heli ... [vv.24–37: through David, Abraham and Noah] ... 38 son of Enos, son of Seth, son of Adam, son of God.

3. Context

Luke names seven of the most powerful men of his day — the emperor, the governor, the kings and the high priests — and then says God’s word didn’t come to any of them. It came to John, out in the wilderness, who told people to turn back to God (repent) and be baptised. Luke then tells us Jesus was baptised too, and finishes with a long family tree that goes all the way back to Adam, “the son of God”. Our church is 157 years old this week, so this passage asks: are we still listening to God, and is our faith really our own?

4. Discuss

  1. v.1–2 — Luke lists seven powerful men, then says “God’s word came to John … in the wilderness.” Why does Luke write it this way? Who do we think really shapes the world?
  2. v.3 — Repentance means turning around, not just feeling bad. What’s the difference between saying “sorry” and actually turning? Think of an example from home or school.
  3. v.7–9 — Why was “We have Abraham as our father” so dangerous? What are our versions — “my parents are Christians”, “I’ve been at church since I was little”?
  4. v.10–14 — John’s “fruit” is ordinary: share, be honest, don’t take what isn’t yours. What would real fruit look like at school, on your phone, with money or with your family?
  5. v.15–18 — John has a crowd, yet he points straight to Jesus, “more powerful than I am”. Why is that good news (v.18)? How is it different from chasing followers?
  6. v.21–22 — Jesus never sinned, yet he lined up with sinners to be baptised. What does that tell us about what Jesus is like — and about how we treat people we think are “worse” than us?
  7. v.22 — The Father says, “You are my beloved Son; with you I am well-pleased” — before Jesus has done any ministry. In Christ God says that to us too. How is that different from how grades, sport or likes tell us we’re worth something?
  8. v.23–38 — Jesus’ family tree includes flawed people like Jacob and David. What does that say about God’s grace, and about our church’s 157 years? What do you want to hand on to the next generation?

5. Live It Out

KOG 3·2·1·0 — pray for 3, connect with 2, invite 1, 0 left unloved.

  • Pray for 3 — three friends or family members who don’t know Jesus yet. Pray for them by name every day.
  • Connect with 2 — be a real friend this week: a lunch, a walk or a proper chat (not just a reaction in the group chat).
  • Invite 1 — invite one of your three to KOGYouth, to church on Sunday or to the Christmas concert on 13 December.
  • 0 left unloved — spot the person who’s on their own at school or at youth, and sit with them.

This week’s challenge: 10 minutes of “wilderness time” each day — phone in another room, read a bit of Luke 3 and pray. Then pick one “fruit” from v.10–14 and do it.

Memory verse: “You are my beloved Son; with you I am well-pleased.” (Luke 3:22)

6. Pray

  • Thank God that his word still comes to ordinary people, and for 157 years of his faithfulness to our church.
  • Ask God to turn us around where we’ve been coasting, and to help us bear real fruit at school and at home.
  • Pray by name for your 3 friends — that they would hear the Father’s welcome in Jesus.

7. Leader’s Notes

Framing. Joyfully point to Jesus. Some students may not yet be Christians, or may be Christians only because of their parents; this passage is good news for them — the Father’s welcome is given in Jesus, not earned. Never put anyone on the spot, and let students pass on a question if they want to.

Two likely wrong turns

Wrong turn 1 — “try harder.” Students may hear “produce fruit” as a to-do list to earn God’s approval. The sermon was clear that the fruit flows from grace: John’s tax collectors and soldiers changed because God had touched them, not because they gathered merit. Keep coming back to Jesus.

Wrong turn 2 — “I’m fine because of my family.” Students from Christian families may quietly think v.8 is about other people. Gently ask: have I turned to Christ for myself? A long history, or Christian parents, is a responsibility, not insurance.

Question helps

Q1 — God’s history isn’t written by those on thrones but by those who hear his word in the wilderness. The wilderness is a place set apart from the noise of the world — for us, it often means putting the phone down.

Q2 — The Greek word for repentance means a change of mind and a shift of direction. You can cry and say “I was wrong” and then walk the same path; repentance means turning from our own path to God’s.

Q3 — Bloodline, tradition and church identity can’t replace our own relationship with God. God could raise children for Abraham from stones. Heritage is a blessing, but it can become a judgment if we inherit only a religious label.

Q4 — Repentance is not an abstract feeling but a visible change in how we use money, power and how we treat people in need. It flows from God’s free grace, not our own willpower.

Q5 — John points away from himself to the One who is coming. The “good news” (v.18) is Jesus. We are not the main character — he is.

Q6 — Jesus had no sin, yet he stood beside sinners in the Jordan: he identified with them and began the mission that ends at the cross, where he was “numbered with the transgressors” (Isaiah 53:12). True holiness isn’t staying away from sinners; it’s entering their situation and bringing them to God.

Q7 — Heaven opened while Jesus was praying; the Father’s words echo Psalm 2:7 and Isaiah 42:1. This is the identity every Christian receives in Christ — we are loved because we are united with Jesus, not because of what we have achieved.

Q8 — Matthew traces Jesus to Abraham (Israel’s Messiah); Luke traces him to Adam (Saviour of all humanity, the “last Adam”, 1 Corinthians 15:45). The line includes flawed people — it is a genealogy of grace, not heroes. Our 157 years is a testimony to what God has done through weak people. (Luke’s line runs through David’s son Nathan, not Solomon.)

Church vision tie-in
