Home Groups (adults)

An Upside-Down World, a Solid Foundation

Luke 6:20–49 · Sunday 22 November 2026 · St Paul’s Anglican Church Kogarah · For adult home groups · Sermon by Rev Jerry Zhu

Big Idea: The kingdom of God does not belong to the so-called “self-sufficient”, but to those who know they have nothing and can only depend on God; so build your identity and security on Jesus Christ, and let love, forgiveness, generosity and obedience flow from the grace you have already received.

Aim: by the end of the study, group members will

see that Jesus’ blessings and woes turn the world’s values upside down, and ask honestly what they are building their identity and security on;

understand that loving enemies, not judging and giving generously flow from who we already are in Christ (identity before behaviour), not from moral self-effort;

name one place where their foundation is being tested, and take one concrete step of forgiveness or generosity this week, resting on what Christ has already done.

You’ll need: Bibles (CSB), pens, about 60–75 minutes.

1. Connect (10 min)

  • Imagine stopping someone outside Kogarah station and asking, “What kind of person is blessed?” What answers would you expect? Which would you have given ten years ago, and which now?
  • Think of a time someone wronged you at work, in the family or over the back fence. What was your first instinct, and what did you actually do?

2. Read: Luke 6:20–49 (CSB)

3. Context

This is Luke’s “Sermon on the Plain”. After a night of prayer and choosing the Twelve, Jesus comes down to a level place and, looking up at his disciples, tells them what following him really means. It parallels the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7), but Luke’s version is sharper and more direct, setting blessings and woes side by side. Jesus is not handing out “chicken soup for the soul”: he is announcing that the kingdom of God has come, and it turns the world’s values upside down.

4. Discuss

  1. (vv.20–23) Luke tells us Jesus spoke “looking up at his disciples”. Who are these blessings for? Matthew has “poor in spirit”; Luke has “you who are poor”. How do the two fit together?
  2. (vv.20–23) The sermon described a “middle-class spiritual mindset”: God owes me something; I have earned my standing by hard work. Where do you see that mindset in yourself, at work, in parenting, or even in serving at church?
  3. (vv.24–26) Is Jesus cursing the rich? What does “you have received your comfort” mean? The sermon called these woes “not a punishment but a diagnosis”. What might Jesus diagnose in our suburb, and in you?
  4. (vv.27–30) What do “offer the other also” and “don’t hold back your shirt” mean (see Exodus 22:26–27)? Now read John 18:22–23. How do we tell a heart free of revenge from simply putting up with harm?
  5. (vv.31–36) How is v.31 different from “you’re good to me, so I’ll be good to you”? In v.35, do we become children of the Most High by loving our enemies, or love our enemies because we are his children? Why does the order matter?
  6. (v.37) What is the difference between judging and discerning (John 7:24)? Where are we tempted to sit on the judge’s bench, and what do we rename it (“I’m just being honest”, “righteous anger”, “sharing a prayer point”)?
  7. (v.38) Picture the grain seller’s measure: pressed down, shaken together, running over. What does it tell us about God? What does “the measure you use” look like in a workplace, a family feud or a dispute with a neighbour?
  8. (vv.39–49) Why do the two houses look the same until the flood? What is the difference between “trying harder to obey” and building on the righteousness of Christ? Where is your foundation being tested right now?

  • Connect with 2 — a coffee, a meal or a walk with two of them this week. Listen for what they are building their security on.
  • Invite 1 — invite one of your three to our Community Christmas Concert in December (we are praying for 120+), to the Welcome Home — Discover course, or to Christianity Explored (in Mandarin).
  • 0 left unloved — at morning tea, look for the person on the edge, and for the one you find hardest to love, and be generous with your welcome.

This week’s challenge: Think of the person you find hardest to forgive. Each day, before you pray for them by name (v.28), meditate on Romans 5:8: Christ died for you while you were still his enemy. Then ask God for one good thing you can do for them, where it is safe and wise.

Memory verse: “Why do you call me ‘Lord, Lord,’ and don’t do the things I say?” (Luke 6:46)

6. Pray

  • Thank God that his kingdom belongs to the poor, and that his grace, given at infinite cost, is our only sure foundation. Confess where we have built on wealth, comparison or other people’s praise.
  • Ask for hearts that love enemies, refuse to judge and give generously, out of what we have already received. Pray for three new mid-week Growth Groups, and for the Community Christmas Concert in December.
  • Pray by name for your 3: that they would see their need of God and find their security in Jesus, and for an opportunity to invite one of them.

7. Leader’s Notes

Framing. Joyfully point to Jesus. Guests and not-yet-Christians may be present; never corner anyone. This passage is easily heard as moralism: “try harder to be poor, to love, to give.” The sermon’s key is that identity comes before behaviour: we love, forgive and give not to earn anything, but because we have already received. Some in your group may be “poor, hungry and weeping” right now (a lost job, a relationship, a dream); let them hear v.20 as good news: they are not forgotten.

Question helps

Q1 — Jesus speaks directly to his followers, with certainty, love and seriousness: “You need to know what following me really means.” No contradiction: the poverty includes both material and spiritual poverty. Those with nothing to lean on often find it easiest to admit they need God. To be poor in spirit is to see we cannot save ourselves, and to receive God’s free grace.

Q2 — The sermon’s example: a Christian manager made redundant as Australian banks cut around 7000 jobs in the shift to AI. He saw that his “riches” were a mindset, “I am enough on my own”, and losing his job let him become truly “poor” before God. Explore gently where we feel God owes us.

Q3 — No: Abraham, Job and Nicodemus were wealthy. The woe is on those who make wealth their comfort, ultimate security and identity: they have their comfort now and nothing in eternity. The sermon’s examples: a young man filling his heart with social-media comparison (v.25), and a sister who needed praise for her service (v.26). A life built on what death, the market, illness and old age can take is already “woe”.

Q4 — The coat was a night blanket that had to be returned by sunset; it stands for livelihood and dignity, the cheek for reputation. Jesus calls us out of the cycle of revenge, entrusting both to the Father as Judge and provider. But at his trial Jesus did not turn the other cheek (John 18:22–23): this is a posture of the heart, not a rigid rule. With ongoing domestic violence, crime or protecting the innocent, seeking justice and setting boundaries fit Scripture. Be pastorally alert here.

Q5 — The world: first receive, then give. Jesus: give first, expect nothing back. We don’t become God’s children by loving enemies; we already are his children in Christ, so we can live out his likeness, gracious to the ungrateful and evil. Reverse the order and it becomes moralism; keep it and it is a gospel ethic (Romans 5:8). Sermon example: praying for someone being “cancelled” online instead of joining the outrage.

Q6 — Christians must discern right from wrong. Jesus forbids self-righteous, condemning, unforgiving judgement. Its root is forgetting that we too are sinners saved by grace; judging says, “I don’t need to be forgiven the way you do.” The opposite of judging is not “no standards” but humble forgiveness.

Q7 — The generous seller presses, shakes and heaps the grain until it spills over: the buyer receives more than he deserves, as God gives us far beyond what we deserve. “The measure” is not karma but a law of relationships: the stingy live in scarcity, the generous in abundance. Sermon example: the brother at work “you don’t mess with”, who lived in a world where everyone was out to take advantage of him.

Q8 — The foundation is out of sight until the test. Jesus asks not for perfect obedience but for loyalty: if he is Lord, life heads towards doing. The foundation is Christ’s righteousness, not our performance. Sermon example: the brother who couldn’t forgive, whose group leader asked whether he was relying on willpower or on Christ’s forgiveness. Note too the sermon’s challenge to churches: not only “How do we keep people?” but “Are we doing what Christ says?”
