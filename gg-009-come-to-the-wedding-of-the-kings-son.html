Home Groups (adults)

Come to the Wedding of the King's Son

Matthew 22:1–14 · Sunday 6 September 2026 · St Paul's Anglican Church Kogarah · For adult home groups · Preached by Rev David Yung

Big Idea: Don't insult the Heavenly Father's invitation with excuses or defiance — come when he summons, clothed in what he provides.

Aim: (1) See that the Father has already prepared everything, and that the gospel asks only that we come. (2) Name the respectable, good things that have quietly been outranking God's summons. (3) Take one step this week in the 3 · 2 · 1 · 0 rhythm — and invite one person to the Father's Day season at St Paul's.

You'll need: Bibles (CSB), pens, about 60–75 minutes.

Connect (10 min)

  • Tell the group about an invitation you accepted and then didn't honour. What did it cost the person who invited you, and what did you tell yourself at the time?
  • It's Father's Day week. What is one thing your father, or a father figure, did for you that you didn't appreciate until much later?

Read — Matthew 22:1–14 (CSB)

1 Once more Jesus spoke to them in parables: 2 "The kingdom of heaven is like a king who gave a wedding banquet for his son. 3 He sent his servants to summon those invited to the banquet, but they didn't want to come.

4 Again, he sent out other servants and said, 'Tell those who are invited: See, I've prepared my dinner; my oxen and fattened cattle have been slaughtered, and everything is ready. Come to the wedding banquet.'

5 But they paid no attention and went away, one to his own farm, another to his business, 6 while the rest seized his servants, mistreated them, and killed them. 7 The king was enraged, and he sent out his troops, killed those murderers, and burned down their city.

8 Then he told his servants, 'The banquet is ready, but those who were invited were not worthy. 9 Go then to where the roads exit the city and invite everyone you find to the banquet.' 10 So those servants went out on the roads and gathered everyone they found, both evil and good. The wedding banquet was filled with guests.

11 When the king came in to view the guests, he saw a man there who was not dressed for a wedding. 12 So he said to him, 'Friend, how did you get in here without wedding clothes?' The man was speechless.

13 Then the king told the attendants, 'Tie him up hand and foot, and throw him into the outer darkness, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth.'

14 For many are invited, but few are chosen."

Context

Jesus is in the temple courts in the last week before the cross, answering the leaders who have just decided to arrest him. This is the third of three judgment parables in a row (21:28-32; 21:33-46; 22:1-14), each sharper than the last. A royal wedding then was public, days long and hugely expensive, and a king's invitation was not a social nicety but a summons — so guests who had accepted and then declined were withdrawing loyalty from their king.

Discuss

  1. Read verses 1-4. What has the king already done before anyone is asked to come, and what is the only thing asked of the guests? Why does that order matter?
  2. Verse 3 says he sent servants to summon those already invited. What does it tell you about God that he follows up his own people rather than waiting for them to come to their senses?
  3. Verse 5: a farm and a business. These are not sins. Why does Jesus choose respectable excuses rather than obviously wicked ones — and what does that say about how apathy works in a comfortable church?
  4. Verses 6–7 are shocking. Why is the king's response not disproportionate within the world of the parable? What does it tell us about how God regards an insult aimed at his Son?
  5. Verses 8-10: the wedding is not cancelled; only the guest list changes, and "both evil and good" are gathered in. What screening criterion does the King give his servants — and how does that reshape who you would invite?
  6. Verses 11–13: a man is swept in off the street and then put out. What exactly was wrong with him, given that suitable clothing was available? Why is it important that he was already seated inside the feast?
  7. The king calls him "friend" and asks him a question rather than simply throwing him out. Where is the mercy in that, and what does the man's silence tell us?
  8. Verse 14. Look honestly at your own life: is there anything in your week that shows you have actually come to the marriage — or have you mostly kept your name on the guest list?

Live It Out

Our church rhythm is 3 · 2 · 1 · 0, and this passage is its warrant: the servants were sent to invite, not to assess.

  • PRAY FOR 3 — three people you know who aren't Christians yet. Write the names down tonight; take them home on paper.
  • CONNECT WITH 2 — one real conversation each with two of them this week. Neighbours, colleagues, the school gate, morning tea.
  • INVITE 1 — ask one of them to something at St Paul's. "Everything is ready" means you are handing over a completed banquet, not recruiting for a programme.
  • 0 LEFT UNLOVED — at morning tea this Sunday, find the person standing on their own and stay with them for ten minutes.

This week's challenge: Open your diary for this coming week and find your farm and your business — the good, defensible commitment that most reliably outranks God's people and God's word. Move one of them, and tell one other person in the group what you moved.

Memory verse: "Tell those who are invited: See, I've prepared my dinner; my oxen and fattened cattle have been slaughtered, and everything is ready. Come to the wedding banquet." — Matthew 22:4 (CSB)

Pray

  • Thank the Father that everything is ready — that the cost of the feast was met at the cross and nothing is asked of us but to come.
  • Pray by name for your three friends who aren't Christians yet: that God's kindness would lead them to repentance, and that he would give you one opening this week.
  • Pray for our Father's Day Celebration, "A Father's Invitation", and for our home groups — that they would be places where people first hear the King's summons and are gladly clothed in Christ.

Leader's Notes

Lead this joyfully. The parable has fire in it, but the fire is a Father's love for his Son — the aim is not guilt about diaries but gladness about the banquet.

Guests and not-yet-Christians may be in the room, and so may long-standing members who have never actually come. Both are being invited tonight, not exposed. Never corner anyone or ask for a public declaration.

Wrong turn 1 — decoding the wedding robe. Jesus gives no allegory; what he shows is the man's refusal. Clothing was available and he could not be bothered. Preach the refusal, not the symbol.

Wrong turn 2 — letting v.14 become a predestination argument or an anxiety spiral. Matthew's "called" is the wide outward invitation; the chosen are known by an obedient response.

Question helps

  • Q1 — Everything is already ready (v.4); the guests bring only themselves. Preparation precedes summons, and that order is the ground of assurance: you are invited not because you are ready but because he is.
  • Q2 — He is a King who chases. These had already accepted, and he still sends again (cf. Esther 5:8).
  • Q3 — Respectable excuses are the effective ones. Nobody stays away having decided to be wicked; the diary is simply full. Note v.6 — indifference hardens into hostility.
  • Q4 — Shrugging off an accepted royal invitation withdrew loyalty from a patron, so troops are not disproportionate within the story. Privilege can be forfeited (cf. 21:43).
  • Q5 — "As many as you find" — no criterion at all. We do not vet people before we ask them (cf. Acts 13:46). Expect the response from those with least to protect.
  • Q6 — Contempt, not poverty; and he was already seated inside. Being in the hall is not the same as being fit for the King. Judas was in the group, at the supper, and lost.
  • Q7 — "Friend" is a real appeal, not only irony. His silence is his own verdict. The mercy is that a question is asked at all — while it is still being asked, there is time to answer.
  • Q8 — Push towards evidence rather than sentiment: a diary, a wallet, a tongue, a Sunday.
