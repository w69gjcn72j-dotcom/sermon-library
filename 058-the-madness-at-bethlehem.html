The Madness at Bethlehem

Matthew 2:16-23 · Advent 2018
Big IdeaThe madness of men can never thwart the purposes of God.

Sermon Title: The madness at Bethlehem

Passage: Matthew 2:16-23

Big Idea: The madness of men can never thwart the purposes of God

A mad king rages against the Innocents

Mother Rachel weeps for her sons

Welcome friends, to the Advent Season.

For the Christians, it’s a time for us to remember the first coming of our Lord Jesus Christ.

It’s a time for celebration and thanksgiving.

For some people, it’s a very busy season of Christmas shopping, Christmas parties and Christmas lunches.

To help those of us who prepare presents and dinners at the last possible minute, shopping centres would open continuously for a crazy long period just before Christmas.

I remember a few years ago, some Westfield Shopping Centres would open for 36 hours straight, right through to late Christmas Eve. Ppt

People would call this the mad rush before Christmas.

In today’s passage, we’ll see a madness that’s associated with Christmas.

But it’s a madness that’s far more sinister for it’s a madness that rages against the innocents.

A mad king rages against the Innocents Ppt

Please open up your Bible to Matthew 2:16.

“Then Herod, when he realized that he had been outwitted by the wise men, flew into a rage.”

Herod was in a rage; he was furious!

Why was he furious? We’ll need to go back to the beginning of the chapter to find out.

At the beginning of Matthew chapter two, we see wise men from the East coming into the City of Jerusalem.

They’re saying, “Where is he who has been born king of the Jews?”

Herod’s disturbed when he heard the news.

Why? Because he’s the King of the Jews!

He wants to find out more about this new King that the wise men are talking about.

And so he asks his chief priests and scribe about where the Christ would be born.

Christ? Shouldn’t he be looking for where the King would be born? Why is he looking for where the Christ would be born?

It’s because in the Old Testament prophecies, God has promised that the Anointed One, the Christ, will be his Special King.

This Christ, this Special King, would be a powerful King, saving God’s people and ruling over nations.

Herod made the connection that the king that the wise men are seeking is the Christ that God has foretold in the Old Testament.

Now hold that thought! We’re going to revisit this later on.

He finds out that the Christ would come from Bethlehem and so he sends the wise men on the way.

He wants to find this Christ too, so that he can also worship Him.

The wise men eventually find the Child and his family, but God warns them in a dream not to go back to Herod.

And so, they go home via a different route.

Meanwhile, the young family is told by an Angel of the Lord to escape to Egypt.

When Herod finds out what happens, he flies into a fit of rage.

He’s furious that the wise men had outsmarted him!

He’s frustrated that his plan to visit and destroy the Christ-Child won’t succeed.

And so what does he do? He responds with utter ruthlessness.

He massacres all the boys in and around Bethlehem two years old and under.

Imagine the horror of having your two year old son being ripped away from his parents’ loving arms and slaughtered by Herod’s henchmen. Ppt

Unfortunately, history tells us that this is what you can expect from a mad king like Herod.

History tells us that Herod’s fear of losing his power was so great it led him to murder his mother-in-law, his favourite wife, three of his sons, and the High Priest.

300 of his palace officials who displeased him also lost their lives.

As he was dying, he gave orders that on his death, the leading member of every family in Jerusalem should be executed, so as to make sure that throughout the land, there would be genuine grief at his passing.

No one would cry for Herod and so he made sure that there was at least someone crying on the day of his death, even if the tears weren’t for him.

Thankfully, as far as we know, that order wasn’t carried out.

Herod was a mad king.

He had no regard for human life; he had no regard for the life of the Innocent Boys.

And he had no regard for the life of the Christ!

Remember what I said before about what Herod thought of the Christ?

He knew that God had promised a King who would save God’s people and who would rule over many nations.

And what did Herod do?

He wanted to kill the Christ! He would rather that the Christ, the Son of God be killed, then to have his authority threatened.

You see, Herod was the King of the Jews, but he wasn’t even a Jew.

Herod got his kingship because of his political dealings with the Romans.

He was never a legitimate King of the Jews.

He wasn’t a Jew and he wasn’t from the line of David.

He had no right to be the King of God’s people; he had no right to be the King of the Jews.

And yet, he wanted to secure his false claim to the throne so much that he would murdered and killed.

His madness for the throne drove him to want to kill the Christ, the Saviour of the world.

Imagine that, knowingly trying to kill the Saviour of the world!

In order to have his security, He ruthlessly killed the innocent boys of Bethlehem, hoping to kill the Christ in the process.

He sacrificed these innocent boys for a greater sense of security.

Sadly, since then, many more innocent children had been sacrificed for a greater sense of security.

In America alone, 44.5 million innocent children have been aborted over the past fifty years.

When asked why these parents would abort their children, 40% said that they were financially unprepared.

36% said it was bad timing; 31% said that there was an issue with their partner.

I know that having children can be expensive and that when you have children there may be things that you can’t do anymore.

You might not be able to afford the house that you want or the lifestyle that you strive for.

I’m sure all responsible parents can attest that there will be times when you have to ask yourself how you ever going to raise these children to adulthood.

But to sacrifice your child’s life so that your life is more secure and more manageable?

Isn’t that what Herod was doing?

Now, there might be some of us who knows someone for whom this is a very difficult issue.

You might know some people who is undergoing very difficult circumstances and having a baby will make life much more difficult.

And I don’t want to trivialise whatever difficulty that they might be going through.

However, can I implore you to remember how precious each person’s life is before God’s eyes.

Each person’s life is important, no matter how small.

Don’t be a Herod; don’t sacrifice innocent lives for the sake of security.

If you know a couple who’s finding it difficult to have the baby, help them in whatever way you can.

Support them both in prayer and practical support.

Help them to raise their child together; let them know that they’re not alone.

Herod’s massacre was not the first time in the Bible where a mad king decides to kill innocent boys.

Do you remember another time when a king decided to kill many innocent boys?

It was back in Exodus, when Pharaoh murdered all the infant boys of the Hebrews.

Many, many innocent boys were killed except for one.

His name was Moses, who would one day rescue his people from Egypt, the land of slavery.

Here in the New Testament, many innocent boys of Bethlehem were killed, except for one.

And his name was Jesus; he too will one day rescue his people, not from Egypt, but from an even more oppressive force.

He will one day save his people from their sin.

In fact, this whole account of Jesus’ birth was like a mini-version of the Old Testament.

The infant Hebrew boys were killed by Pharaoh; the infant boys of Bethlehem were killed by mad Herod.

The Israelites came out of Egypt and went to Israel; Jesus would later on in the chapter come out of Egypt and go back to Israel.

Matthew then fast-forwards us to another period in Israel’s history and shows us how the events surrounding Jesus’ birth fulfils a prophecy.

He takes us to the time of the exiles and shows fulfilment of a prophecy about Mother Rachel weeping for her sons.

Mother Rachel weeps for her sons

Look with me at verses 17-18,

“17 Then what was spoken through Jeremiah the prophet was fulfilled:

18 A voice was heard in Ramah,

weeping,[f] and great mourning, Rachel weeping for her children; and she refused to be consoled, because they are no more.”

Rachel was the wife of Jacob in the Old Testament; she was also the mother of Joseph and Benjamin.

Rachel was someone who really wanted to be a mother.

In fact, she said to Jacob her husband, “Give me children or I will die!”

When she was giving birth to Benjamin, she was having difficulties with the labour.

Almost immediately after she gave birth to Benjamin, she died.

While she was known as the beloved wife of Jacob, she was also known as a mother to the descendants of Israel.

The prophecy that Matthew referred to came from the prophet Jeremiah, as recorded in Jeremiah 31:15.

Here, Jeremiah is not saying that Rachel was still alive.

However, figuratively speaking, if she was alive, she would be weeping and mourning for her children at Ramah.

That’s because in the time of exile, the Babylonian Army had used Ramah as a prisoner-of-war camp from which to deport their Israelite captives.

From there, the Israelites would be taken into exile, many of whom would die in a foreign land and never see the land of Israel again.

It Rachel was there at Ramah, she would weep for her children leaving her and refusing to be consoled.

It would’ve been a very bitter experience for her.

If Rachel was alive in Bethlehem at the time of Jesus, she would be weeping again.

This time, she would be mourning for her boys who were lost to the madness of King Herod.

She would’ve wept at Egypt, at Ramah and at Bethlehem.

She would’ve mourned for her children who were lost to the madness of tyrants and to the tragedy of war.

To lose a beloved child would be so painful; I simply can’t imagine it.

Someone who have gone through the pain of losing not their child but their grandchild is Philip Jensen. Ppt

Some of you might know Philip Jensen as a powerful and influential speaker.

I have very high regard for Philip and the gospel work that he’s done in Sydney, particularly in the University of NSW, and all over the world.

What you might not know is that last year, Philip had lost his grandson to cancer.

Here’s an excerpt of an article that Philip wrote reflecting the death of Nathan, his grandson. Ppt

‘The death of a grandson is not simply natural and normal; it is tragic and horrible.’

‘My grandson, Nathan, was as his name indicated, a gift from God.

Thirteen months ago, he and I went fishing down at Port Hacking.

As I taught him how to spin for tailor, he told me of his hopes and plans for education, girlfriends, his church youth group, school Bible Study and discussed with me whether to be a preacher or a Bible translator.

A couple of weeks later the headaches started and he underwent brain surgery. Ppt

A couple of months later we learnt of the heart tumour and the likelihood of death.

I prayed that God would take my three score and ten years in place of his less than one score. But it doesn’t work like that.’

Philip and his family went through the tragic loss of Nathan.

It must have been very painful.

It must have also felt very wrong. A parent shouldn’t attend the funeral of their child.

A grandparent shouldn’t attend the funeral of their grandchild.

It’s just wrong.

That’s how it must have felt at Ramah, when the Israelite exiles were taken from their homes.

That’s how it must have felt at Bethlehem at the time of the Herod’s massacre.

However, in the very chapter about Rachel’s mourning, there is a glimmer of hope.

In the very next verse, in Jeremiah 31:16-17, we read, Ppt

‘Keep your voice from weeping and your eyes from tears, for the reward for your work will come— this is the Lord’s declaration— and your children will return from the enemy’s land. 17 There is hope for your future— this is the Lord’s declaration— and your children will return to their own territory.’

Rachel’s sad song of lament is not the end.

The madness of tyrants won’t be the end.

Sin and death won’t be the end.

There is hope for the future.

Later on in that chapter, in 31:31, we read,

““Look, the days are coming”—this is the Lord’s declaration—“when I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah.”

There will be a new covenant, a new hope.

In this new covenant, this new promise, God will forgive his people’s iniquities and will remember their sins no more.

This new hope will come through Jesus, the Christ-Child who was saved from the Bethlehem massacre.

Even though Philip Jensen would’ve given his life gladly in exchange for his grandson Nathan’s, he knew that he wouldn’t be able to save Nathan.

However, Philip said that there is one and only one who can die for others and he has already done it on a cross two thousand years ago.

While Nathan’s loss was painful, it was not the end.

It was not the end because Nathan has hope in the Lord Jesus Christ.

In the last few months of his life, Nathan made a video in the hospital for his school friends.

He wants his friends to have the same hope that he has in Jesus.

Friends, this is the hope that we want you to have at Christmas.

This is the hope that we want all our family members, all our friends, all the people living in our neighbourhood, to have at Christmas.

Christmas is not just about holidays or presents.

Christmas isn’t just about family get-togethers and Christmas parties.

Having those things at Christmas are good and I hope that you will enjoy them.

But if you don’t have the hope of Jesus, then all these things are meaningless.

Indeed, it’s madness trying to enjoy Christmas without Jesus.

Have you heard of the word, ‘Bedlam’? It is a word that has changed from another word. ‘Bedlam’ is a corruption of the word ‘Bethlehem.’ Ppt

In England, in the year 1247, a hospital was founded that was called ‘St Mary of Bethlehem’.

It was a general hospital for the next 200 years. But then it was decided to turn it into a hospital for the insane. In those days, little was known about how to care for those with a mental illness, so the hospital developed a reputation for noise, uproar and confusion.

The original name of ‘Bethlehem Hospital’ became ‘Bedlam Hospital’, and the word later entered our English language to describe any situation marked by chaos and distress.

Friends, we live in a bedlam world.

We live in a world that’s confused about gender.

We’ve seen how this confusion is spreading throughout our society over the past twelve months.

We live in a world that’s confused about religion.

As early as tomorrow, there will be a debate in parliament that can have a devastating effect on religious freedom in our schools.

I’ve received this news from the Archbishop just two days ago, as I was preparing for this sermon:

‘Bill in the Senate this week which, appropriately, removes the exemption provisions allowing religious schools to discriminate against students but, alarmingly, provides no provision that gives our schools the religious freedom to operate according to a Christian ethos.’

And the Archbishop asks us to pray.

Friends, there is utter madness and bedlam in our society today.

From a human point of view, our future as a believing community might look very grim.

However, even in the midst of the chaos, God is in control.

He’s always been in control, he’ll always be in control.

No plan of man can thwart the purposes of God.

No madness of any king, any government can thwart the purposes of God.

And so, as we think about where our Church is going in the next few years, let’s seek the purposes of God.

Let’s always ask him to guide us as well as for the courage to keep trusting his lead.

In a moment, I’ll speak to us briefly about the vision statement that our Church will be using over the next few years.

However, before that, let’s spend some time praying for those in our government, that they might may wise and brave decisions tomorrow about our religious freedom.

Let’s pray. The first part of my prayer is called a prayer for the Innocents.

It’s a prayer that the Church has been using on the Day of Innocents, traditionally on the 28th December, to remember the Innocent boys who died at Bethlehem.

It’s also a great prayer for us to pray in light of what our government is facing tomorrow.

Let’s pray,

“Almighty God, whose loving purposes cannot be frustrated by the wickedness of men, so that even infants may glorify you by their deaths: strengthen us by your grace, that by the innocency of our lives and the constancy of our faith even to death, we may glorify your holy Name; through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

And Father, in our mad and bedlam world, please strengthen our resolve to trust in you, knowing that your purposes can never be thwarted. Father, we pray for our Parliament this weekend. Father, please change the hearts of people who might have decided to limit our religious freedom. Father, we pray that we might continue to have the freedom to teach the Bible and the Christian faith without fear or favour in our schools and Bible colleges. Father, we pray that our those in authority might rule so that we may live peaceful and quiet lives in all godliness and holiness. For Jesus’ sake, Amen.”
