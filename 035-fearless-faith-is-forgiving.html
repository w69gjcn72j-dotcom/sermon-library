Fearless faith is forgiving

Big Idea Trust in God’s redemption in Christ — promises kept, promises to come — produces heartfelt forgiveness and fearless hope.

1. The Foreshadowing of redemption

2. The Forgiveness given to the fearful

3. The Fearless hope of the faithful

McCrindle Research published a report in April this year about the impact of global events on Australia’s morale and behaviour.

These past six months have seen a few significant events, including the Bondi shooting, the capturing of the Venezuelan president, the unrest in Iran and of course, the fuel crisis that followed.

How do Australians, all of us, whether we’re born here or immigrants, respond to these events?

According to McCrindle Research, one in four Australians don’t believe that the world is a safe place right now.

If we don’t feel safe, we often feel anxious.

In fact, proportionally there are more people feeling anxious and frustrated right now, compared with this time five years ago, in the middle of COVID.

This seems to have a higher effect on those who are younger, particularly the young adults.

One quarter of our young adults feel overwhelmed and 31% of them feel scared.

From speaking with some of the young adults, I empathise with them.

There are a lot of uncertainties ahead.

We might feel helpless about what lies ahead; it might be going down a direction that I don’t want to go.

And so, a young adult, or an adult, or a child, may ask the same question, “How do I deal with my fear about my future?”

That’s what we’re going to look at in today’s passage.

If you have your Bible there, please open up to Genesis 50.

We’re going to see three things:

1. The Foreshadowing of redemption

2. The Forgiveness given to the fearful

3. The Fearless hope of the faithful

How do we deal with our fears about our future?

Let’s ask God to help us, let’s pray, “Our heavenly Father, please still our hearts as we come before your throne. Take away anything that distracts and help us to hear your still small voice in your word the Bible. Help us to listen to the voice of our Shepherd, the Lord Jesus, in whose name we pray, Amen.”

The Foreshadowing of redemption

We’ve been following Jacob’s journey in the book of Genesis since the start of this year.

We’ve seen how he tricked his father into blessing him instead of his older brother Esau.

We’ve seen him running away from his family, and we’ve seen Jacob the trickster himself being tricked into working for his father-in-law for twenty years.

In time, even his own sons also tricked him into thinking that his favourite son Joseph was dead, when in fact, the brothers sold Joseph into Egypt.

For over twenty years, Jacob believed the lie but in God’s loving providence, it was revealed that Joseph was not only alive, but played an important part under God in saving many people both in Egypt and beyond. For over twenty years, Jacob thought he would go to his grave mourning for the death of his son Joseph. However, in God’s mercy, Jacob would die peacefully and Joseph was there to farewell his father. Look with me at verse one: Then Joseph, leaning over his father’s face, wept and kissed him.

I remember going to the funeral of a father of a friend. My friend didn’t have an easy childhood, and his father didn’t make it easy for him when my friend got married. Even so, my friend and his wife, who’s also a good friend and a wonderful Christian sister, trusted God and did all they could under God to honour my friend’s father. At the funeral, my friend, despite all the hardship he’s gone through, still showed his love for his father. A tender moment was when he took his very young daughter down the driveway to say goodbye to her ‘Ye Ye’. It was a beautiful moment to experience.

Despite all the turmoil in Jacob’s life, Joseph his son loved, respected and honoured him. Joseph wept for his father. Joseph wept for his father when he came down to Egypt to see him. He wept for his father again at his death. Joseph then ordered that his father be embalmed. In ancient Egypt, Pharaohs and other important people were embalmed to preserve the dead body. They would remove the organs, and soak the bodies with special salts to preserve the body. That is, they mummified Jacob; they made Jacob’s dead body into a mummy. It took the Egyptians forty days to complete the embalmment process. After that they would put the mummified body of Jacob into a sarcophagus, as they usually did for other mummies. In fact, you can see an example of a sarcophagus in the University of Sydney. Why did Joseph ask Jacob to be mummified? The Egyptians believed that if you make the body of a dead person into a mummy, they would have a better afterlife. However, Joseph didn’t mummify his father Jacob because he believed in what the Egyptians thought about the journey into the afterlife. No, it was because he was preparing his father for another journey, as commanded by his father Jacob. Look with me at Genesis 47:29-30, “When the time approached for him to die, he called his son Joseph and said to him, “If I have found favor with you, put your hand under my thigh and promise me that you will deal with me in kindness and faithfulness. Do not bury me in Egypt. 30 When I rest with my ancestors, carry me away from Egypt and bury me in their burial place.” Jacob didn’t want to be buried in Egypt; he wanted to be buried in Canaan. His grandfather Abraham already bought a place there. This was the only place in the world that Abraham and his family could call theirs at that point. But more importantly, this place was in the land that God has promised to give to Abraham and his family. Egypt would not be his final resting place; God’s promised land would be his final resting place. And so, in keeping with the promise that Joseph swore to his father, Joseph prepared and mummified his father to take his father back to Canaan, the Promised Land.

Joseph asked Pharaoh’s household to convey that he had sworn to bury his father Jacob in Canaan and so he asked for Pharaoh’s permission through them. Pharaoh gave Joseph permission to go back to Canaan so that he could honour his oath to his father. Joseph then proceeded to take Jacob’s mummified body back and a lot of people went with him. There were three groups of people. The first group was all Pharaoh’s servants, the elders of his household and all the elders of the land of Egypt. These were the elites of Egyptian society; they were at the very top of the Egyptian social ladder. Over the years, they’ve come to respect Joseph, the man who saved them from starvation. They came with him to show their respect to Joseph’s father. Next came the family, including Joseph’s family, his brothers and his father’s family. Only the little ones and the flocks were left behind in Goshen. Finally, there were the horses and chariots, the military. They were there to protect many important Egyptian officials who were travelling with Joseph to Canaan.

This was a massive funeral procession. There would’ve been hundreds of people travelling with Joseph. If they started from Cairo, it would’ve been over 500kms away. It was the grandest state funeral in all of the Bible. But you know what? This was a preview for something bigger. What could be bigger than this? Let’s take a summary of what was happening in these few verses. Joseph asked Pharaoh for permission to leave Egypt. Pharaoh gave permission. Joseph and his family left Egypt. Important Egyptian officials and, more importantly, horses and chariots of the Egyptian army, came out of Egypt, exiting Egypt, with Joseph’s family.

Doesn’t that sound similar to another account in the Bible, where a group of people left Egypt and horses and chariots followed them? Yes, it sounded a lot like what happened in Exodus, the second book of the Bible. In Exodus, we would see that Jacob’s family grew exponentially. They became such a large population that the Egyptians were threatened by them and so they made them into slaves. God then sent Moses to ask Pharaoh for permission to leave. The new Pharaoh wouldn’t and so God sent plagues to Egypt. Unlike what happened with Jacob’s funeral procession, the Israelites didn’t leave their children in Goshen but took them, as well as their livestock and all that they had. However, Pharaoh changed his mind and sent horses and chariots after the Israelites, not to protect them but to force them to return to Egypt.

And so, this grand funeral procession of Israel was itself a foreshadowing of the redemption that God would bring about for the Israelites, about four hundred years after the funeral. Even the Canaanites, the people whom the Israelites would fight against in the Promised Land, got a mention here. Look with me at verse 11, ‘“When the Canaanite inhabitants of the land saw the mourning at the threshing floor of Atad, they said, “This is a solemn mourning on the part of the Egyptians.” Therefore the place is named Abel-mizraim. It is across the Jordan.’ The word Abel-Mizraim means the Mourning of Egypt. They would’ve seen a lot of Egyptians crying and assumed that it was for one of their own. However, the weeping was for Jacob, whose family had been living among the Canaanites as strangers for three generations and would be coming back many generations afterwards.

Over the past few weeks, as we read through the final chapters of Genesis, and as we read about Jacob’s funeral, we might think that everything is coming to a finale, a conclusion. And of course, it’s true to a certain extent. Jacob was buried in his final resting place. However, the Bible here wants us to widen our view to get a better glimpse of reality, not just in the moments we live in, but in the years and the centuries that came before us and will come after us. Life as we know it doesn’t end when our life ends. Life will continue, but it will continue without us. Take Jacob for example. He had a very full life. He was blessed in many ways but he also made many mistakes. But as he grew, he realised that life, even his life, wasn’t all about him. He was part of something bigger. He was the grandson of Abraham, the one through whom God promised to bless the whole world. It would be through Jacob’s line that God would fulfil that promise. Though it was certainly a good thing for him to see that God was starting to fulfil his promises, by giving him a large family and a wonderful son in Joseph, it wasn’t quite what God had promised. God promised nations and land, and here he was, an old man dying as a stranger in a foreign country.

But Jacob didn’t give up on God. For a dying man who didn’t see God’s fulfilment of his promises, he was hopeful. It was because he knew that God was faithful in keeping his promises. God’s promises would still stand, they would still happen even if Jacob didn’t get to see it in his lifetime. Being able to step back, to see what God’s done in the past, and to look back in his life to see what God’s done for him, was what gave Jacob hope. The same goes for us as well. In a world where there are so many uncertainties, so many risks, we want to hold onto something that’s solid, something that gives us certainties. For some of us it might be a good mark at school or a stable job. For others, it might be good friendships. If I have good friends, then I’ll be fine. Others will say, if I have good health and live a long life, then my life would be good. And so, when these are taken away, when we don’t do well at school, when we lose our job, when my friends abandon, or worse, betray me, when I’m ill, we fall into despair. However, for those of us who are Christians, we can, with God’s help, take a step back. We step back and see and give thanks to how God’s kept his promises to us in the past. We know that there’s more to life than just our lives and that there is more to God’s plan than we could possibly ever understand. And so, we step back, and we put our trust in Him. God made a promise; he will keep it. I might not see it in the next few years, I might not even see it in my lifetime, but God will make it happen.

Over the past two weeks, I’ve been visiting Mr Guo at the hospital. The hospital called me because Mr Guo was hoping to see a Cantonese-speaking minister. I didn’t know Mr Guo before I went but I visited him and I got the opportunity to share the gospel with him. He shared that he heard about Christianity before but he didn’t want to commit. Now, in possibly his final days, he wanted to believe. Praise God, he prayed to accept Jesus. Later on in the week, I went back and had the privilege of baptising Mr Guo, at his request. When I spoke to him, his main concern wasn’t about his condition. He didn’t ask for prayers of healing. He asked that he might be holy, and he had tears of joy running down his cheeks, which he described as tears which washed away his guilt. He asked about how to pray to Jesus. And he asked for his family to believe in Jesus. Mr Guo may only have days left, but he has hope. In times of deep sadness, Mr Guo took a step back and started to run to God. He knew that knowing the love of Jesus is more precious than life itself. That’s what hope looks like.

How about you? Where do you place your hope? Is all your hope, all your dreams, contained in this moment before you, contained to this life that you’re living? Or is your hope bigger? Like Jacob, like Mr Guo, take a step back and put your hope in God.

The Forgiveness given to the fearful

After Joseph buried his father Jacob in the land of Canaan, he and his brothers headed back to Egypt. After the death of their father, a dark thought was forming in the brothers’ minds. Look with me at verse 15, “When Joseph’s brothers saw that their father was dead, they said to one another, “If Joseph is holding a grudge against us, he will certainly repay us for all the suffering we caused him.” They knew that what they did to Joseph was bad. It was so bad, that in their minds, it’s unforgiveable. They sold their little brother into slavery. What kind of brother does that? And what kind of brother would forgive someone who’s done that? Joseph hugged them and things had been going well for the past seventeen years, while their father Jacob was still alive. But now that Jacob was dead, how would Joseph treat them? Wouldn’t he repay the brothers for what they’ve done to him? And so, they sent a message to Joseph saying that their father Jacob told their brothers to tell Joseph this, as recorded in verse 17, ‘Say this to Joseph: Please forgive your brothers’ transgression and their sin—the suffering they caused you.’ There was no record in the Bible that Jacob actually said this to the brothers. For all of these seventeen years, there was no record that Jacob would’ve suspected that Joseph would take revenge on his brothers. And so, there’s a possibility that the brothers made this command up as well, hoping that Joseph would spare their lives. In light of what Jacob their father allegedly commanded, the brothers said, “Therefore, please forgive the transgression of the servants of the God of your father.” They didn’t make excuses for what they’ve done. They sinned, they transgressed. When the brothers came to see Joseph, they even offered themselves as slaves to Joseph. They were very anxious for their future and they would rather take the initiative to make themselves slaves than for Joseph to decide something worse for their punishment.

It’s a little like this. A man once walked to the florist to get some flowers for his wife. It wasn’t Valentine’s Day and it wasn’t Mother’s Day. And so, the florist asked, “Is it for your Anniversary?” “No” “It’s for her birthday then?” “No, not her birthday either”. The customer waiting behind the man then asked, “What did you do this time?” I’m sure there are men who give flowers to their wives for no reason, but for many, if it’s not for a special occasion, then it’s probably for something wrong that the man had done.

That’s what the brothers were doing. They knew that they deserved to be punished for what they did to Joseph and so, they were ready to offer themselves as slaves to Joseph as punishment for their crime. When Joseph heard their message, Joseph wept. This is the second time he wept. He wept for the death of his father and he also wept at what the brothers thought of him. This wasn’t the first time that Joseph wept for his brothers. He wept in secret when he was formally introduced to Benjamin, he wept openly when he revealed himself to them. He wept here because his brothers thought that Joseph was still holding a grudge against them. Joseph had treated them and their families kindly over the past seventeen years. Why did they still think that he would hurt them? Joseph could’ve wept because he felt bad that his brothers still didn’t understand that his love and concern for them was genuine. However, I think it’s far more likely that he was weeping for them, that for all this time, they were still held by the fear that one day, Joseph would punish them. He wept that his brothers had been living with this anxiety about their future the whole time.

Look with me at verse 19 to see Joseph’s response. “But Joseph said to them, “Don’t be afraid. Am I in the place of God?” Joseph reassured them by saying “Don’t be afraid”. They needed to take this command from Jacob seriously. Do Not Be Afraid. Why? It’s because though Joseph was powerful, he was not God. And Joseph had no desire to play God, to dish out divine justice against his brothers. God is the One who dishes out divine justice, not Joseph.

Secondly, it was God’s plan all along. Look with me at verse 20, “You planned evil against me; God planned it for good to bring about the present result—the survival of many people.” There was no doubt that the brothers intentionally plotted evil against Joseph. What they did was bad. However, at the same time, it was God’s plan for good. God is so powerful, so wise and so loving, that he used their evil act to bring about good for Joseph, to Egypt, to the region around Egypt and to Joseph’s brothers themselves. Their act, though evil, was all part of God’s plan to save tens of thousands of people who were living in the region at that time. Though what they did to Joseph was bad, through a long chain of events under God’s providence, tens of thousands survived a devastating famine.

So yes, what they did was evil, but God had planned it for good. And so, there’s no need to be afraid. Joseph wouldn’t jeopardise their future or the future of their children. They would be safe. Look with me at the last sentence of verse 21, “Therefore don’t be afraid. I will take care of you and your children.” And he comforted them and spoke kindly to them.” Literally, Joseph comforted them and spoke to their hearts. Their hearts were troubled, their hearts were broken over what they’ve done to their brother, and so Joseph spoke to their hearts. Joseph wanted their heart of hearts to know that he’s genuinely forgiven them, and has no desire to punish them.

How does Joseph give such heartfelt forgiveness to his brothers? Like Jacob his father, Joseph was able to step back and look at what God has done. Joseph didn’t have all the answers when his brothers threw him into a well, he didn’t know what would happen when he was falsely imprisoned in an Egyptian jail. It would not have made any sense to Joseph why he was going through so many dark times in his life. But looking back on his life now, he could see clearly that it was God who planned it, it was God who brought him through all the difficulties, for the greater good. It was because Joseph could see God’s hands in his life, in the good times, and also in the bad times, that Joseph could forgive his brothers with a heartfelt forgiveness. Joseph could forgive because he trusted in the God whose plan was far bigger than Joseph himself.

As fallen, imperfect people, we feel the impact of sin in our lives, every day. Some of us might feel the weight of our sins on ourselves, feeling that we deserve all the bad things that are happening to us. Indeed, we know that we deserve even more bad things than even the bad things we’re experiencing now. Others might feel the weight of sin that others have committed against us. And we might be tempted to think that they deserve far more bad things in their lives right now. This weight of sin from both within ourselves and outside of ourselves weighs us down, and we begin to spiral down into a deep void, not believing that change is possible, not believing that forgiveness is possible. The deeper we fall into this void, the greater the fear of death and hopelessness grow in our lives. This fear saps life away from us, making our hearts hard, refusing forgiveness and refusing the will to forgive.

When sin weighs on our hearts, we need to take a step back and see the kindness and the love of God. God, who sees all of our hearts, who knows all of our weaknesses, who knows every one of our evil acts, and yet, he still loves us. Joseph trusted in God’s plan of redemption, even though he didn’t know the full story. That’s what you do with someone you trust. You don’t fully understand him but you will still go with him anyway, because you trust him. God has shown himself to be trustworthy, again and again. He’s kept his promises to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, he will keep all the promises that are still yet to come. God’s ultimate fulfilment of his promises to us is Jesus, for the Bible tells us in 1 Corinthians 1:20, “For every one of God’s promises is “Yes” in him. Therefore, through him we also say “Amen” to the glory of God.” Every promise of God finds its yes in Jesus. When someone puts their trust in Jesus, God says Yes to that person’s salvation, God says ‘Yes’ to forgiving that person. When we put our trust in Jesus, God lifts the weight of sin from our back and tosses it as far as the east is from the west. A person who trusts in Jesus is forgiven by God. And someone who is forgiven by God will trust Jesus when he says, “For if you forgive others their offenses, your heavenly Father will forgive you as well. 15 But if you don’t forgive others, your Father will not forgive your offenses.” (Matthew 6:14-15). That’s why Christians can and ought to forgive even the worst of sinners, because they themselves trust in God and have been forgiven by God. The one who trusts in God forgives and they forgive wholeheartedly.

Can you imagine a world where Christians forgive because they have taken a step back and see God’s hand in redemption, especially in the Person of Jesus?

Can you imagine a world where the worst of sinners can find forgiveness from Christians who have themselves been forgiven?

Friends, don’t imagine it. Live it. Live and breathe forgiveness, so that the world around you, the fearful world around you, will see a glimpse of the glory of God’s redemption for us in Jesus.

3. The Fearless hope of the faithful

At the end of Genesis chapter 50, the time had come for Joseph himself to die. Joseph lived 110 years. He got to see his children and grandchildren. What a joy it would’ve been for Joseph to see them growing up in their youth, something that his own father didn’t get to see when he was growing up. The Bible said in Proverbs 17:6, “Grandchildren are the crown of the elderly, and the pride of children is their fathers.”

And so, at this church we love having children in our service, especially when they come with their grandparents and their parents. It’s good to hear the voices of children, not when they’re talking with one another when people are talking at the front, but when they are actively participating in our Church, either by their ministries or simply by their presence. They bring the fathers and mothers, and grandfathers and grandmothers, great joy and pride.

As Joseph was about to die, he gave these instructions to his brothers, in verse 24, “Joseph said to his brothers, “I am about to die, but God will certainly come to your aid and bring you up from this land to the land he swore to give to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. So Joseph made the sons of Israel take an oath: “When God comes to your aid, you are to carry my bones up from here.”

Like his father Jacob, Joseph’s heart didn’t belong to Egypt. It belonged to the land that God has promised to his fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac and to Jacob. And so, like Jacob, he made his brothers take an oath to take his bones back to Canaan, the Promised Land. And like Jacob, after he died at the age of 110, they embalmed Joseph, made him into a mummy, and placed him in a coffin in Egypt. Now notice that there was no mention of a large funeral procession, like the one that Jacob had. The Book of Genesis simply left it as the embalmment of Joseph. Why? It’s because it’s not time yet for Joseph to be buried in Canaan. A time will come when God comes to the aid of the Household of Jacob. God will come and help them leave Egypt. When that time comes, then Joseph asked his brothers to take his bones on their last journey back to the Promised Land.

Like Jacob, Joseph’s vision of what God does is far bigger than even his lifespan. Joseph didn’t know how God would come to the aid of this family, but he knew that God would. One day, God would come to the aid of Jacob’s family and bring them back to the land of Canaan. And though he wouldn’t be alive to see it, he wanted his bones to be on that journey with his family. That’s trust in God’s future promises. That’s hope. Joseph had no way of knowing how the Egyptians would treat his family after he died. In time, the Egyptians would mistreat his family and make them their slaves. There was no way that he knew that his family was about to experience years of living in the darkness of human slavery. However, even if Joseph knew that, he could still trust God. God had said that he would keep his promises, then he would do as he said. Even if his family were too weak and needed aid to get out of Egypt, God would provide the aid.

It was his trust in God that gave Joseph a fearless hope.

There are some people who don’t like the Bible’s teaching of God being in control of everything, that he could be trusted with everything. But I love it! I love it because no matter what happens, no matter how bad things turn out, no matter how hopeless the situation, God is in control. I love it because there’s no situation I find myself in that God hasn’t allowed. That doesn’t mean it’s always nice. There are many situations that I would rather not find myself in, whether it’s because of my own fault or the fault of others. But even in those times, even in the darkest times, even in the most hopeless times, I can trust God. He is completely trustworthy.

After Jesus fed the five thousand men with bread and fish, he sent his disciples to go ahead of him by getting into the boat and sailing across the Lake of Galilee. In the middle of the night, the wind blew and they were straining at the oars. They could hardly keep the boat going. They were not in control of their situation! Suddenly they saw someone walking towards them. They were very much afraid. I’m sure I would be as well. But then Jesus said, in words that are similar to Joseph’s, “Have courage, it is I. Don’t be afraid.” As soon as he got in, the storm calmed down. The winds and the waves obeyed Jesus’ voice. He had absolute authority.

Friends, the winds and the waves still know his voice. He could stop all the storms in your life by whispering a command. The only reason he hasn’t done it is because he has a better plan for you, which needs the waves and the storms there. And so, trust him. If he said he’ll take you to where he is, that is heaven, he will take you there, no matter what happens. It’s because Jesus is the Lord, the Master as well as the Saviour, the Rescuer, that we can have fearless hope even in the midst of life’s life-threatening storms.

How do I deal with my fear about my future?

You do that by first remembering that your future is part of God’s bigger picture of redemption.

Even though we can’t see the whole plan, we trust him.

God has kept his promises to us in the Lord Jesus, and he will keep his promises made in the Lord Jesus that are yet to come.

As people who trust in what he’s done, as people who have received forgiveness from God himself, we can offer heartfelt forgiveness to those who have wronged us.

Heartfelt forgiveness is a fruit of our faith in Jesus.

And finally, we can deal with our fear about our future by having a fearless hope, trusting that all things are in the hands of our heavenly Father.

Trust in God’s redemption in Christ — promises kept, promises to come — produces heartfelt forgiveness and fearless hope.

Three Questions

1. If we’re sure God’s saved us in Christ, why do we still struggle to love and forgive those who've wronged us?

2. How can we anchor our hope to God's promises when, like Joseph's family in Egypt, we may never see it in our lifetime?

3. Think of someone facing an uncertain future. What difference would it make if they have a fearless hope in Jesus?

Trust in God’s redemption in Christ — promises kept, promises to come — produces heartfelt forgiveness and fearless hope.

Father forgive us for not trusting you. Forgive us when we forget that your way is higher than ours. Help us to trust you, especially when we don’t understand, when we go through times of darkness. Teach us to be people who are known for our heartfelt forgiveness and our fearless hope, for we trust in Jesus, in whose name we pray, Amen.”
