In 1999, two researchers, Dunning and Kruger, asked 65 people to judge how funny different jokes are.

What they found was that many people who were bad at telling jokes were also the worst judges of what a good joke should be.

In fact, the researchers concluded those who told the worst jokes often overestimate how good their jokes are.

The bottom 25% predicted themselves to be near the top of the group.

The researchers then studied students going into the final exams and they noticed a similar pattern.

Those who got the lowest mark tended to think that they were better than average.

This pattern is now known as the Dunning-Kruger effect.

The Dunning-Kruger effect is when those who are not the top performers tend to be overconfident in their ability, while those who are actually the top performers think that they’re average or below.

Have you met anyone like that, people who aren’t that good at something thinking they are the best person at it?

In today’s passage, we meet someone like that.

She sees a problem and she thinks she’s the best person to fix it, even though she’s not the most qualified person in the room.

What problem is she trying to fix? Something that God’s said.

Yes, sure, God made a promise, but it doesn’t seem to be working.

It’s good, but it’s not great and it needs fixing. And she’s the person who’s going to fix is.

What happens when she tries to ‘fix’ God’s promises?

Sarai’s fix: an ungodly initiative

Genesis 16:1, “Abram’s wife, Sarai, had not borne any children for him, but she owned an Egyptian slave named Hagar.”

Last week, we read that God has promised Abram that a son would come from Abram’s own body and that through him, God would give Abram many, many sons and daughters.

But there was a problem: Sarai was barren.

It’s now been ten years since God said he would make Abram into a great nation, but Sarai was still barren.

It must have been frustrating and emotionally painful experience for them both, but especially for Sarai.

She was getting desperate.

In fact, she got so desperate that she started to blame God for her condition, in verse 2, ‘Sarai said to Abram, “Since the LORD has prevented me from bearing children, go to my slave; perhaps through her I can build a family.” And Abram agreed to what Sarai said.’

Sarai blames God for not giving her any children and so she found her own solution.

She tells Abram to sleep with Hagar, her Egyptian slave.

Remember when Sarai was taken to Pharoah’s household, most likely to become wife, even though she was married to Abram, and it was Abram who exposed her to that risk?

She probably got Hagar as a slave back then.

Hagar’s now her slave, her property.

He tells Abram to marry her so that she will build her house through Hagar.

That is, she’s getting Abram to sleep with Hagar so that Sarai can take Hagar’s son as her own.

Basically, she’s getting Hagar to be a surrogate mother for her.

A surrogate mother is a woman who bears a baby for someone else.

In 20th Century BC, rich childless women like Sarai would get poor women like Hagar to sleep with their husband to bear a child for them.

In 21st Century AD, rich childless couples also get poorer women to be surrogate mothers, but they do this through specialised clinics.

It’s illegal in most areas in Australia to perform surrogacy for money.

However, it’s legal in other countries around the world.

Sadly, many poor women were exploited as a result, with many women trapped and forced to bear other people’s children against their will.

In Greece last year, a clinic was found guilty of exploiting 169 foreign vulnerable women.

It was a horrible thing to do.

And that’s what Sarai was getting Hagar to do.

Hagar’s her slave; Hagar will do what she was told.

She was getting Hagar to be her surrogate.

And she told her husband what to do: Go sleep with Hagar.

Abram, her husband, will do what he was told.

Verse 3, “So Abram’s wife, Sarai, took Hagar, her Egyptian slave, and gave her to her husband, Abram, as a wife for him. This happened after Abram had lived in the land of Canaan ten years.”

Does this look familiar? Have you seen this before, a wife telling the husband what to do and a woman given as a bride to her husband?

Yes, it happened at the very beginning.

God gave Eve to Adam to be his wife, which has a strange echo in Sarai giving Hagar her slave as a wife to be Abram, Sarai’s husband.

But even more striking is how Abram listened to Sarai to commit a sin, just as Adam listened to Eve to commit a sin.

Eve listened to the snake, ate the forbidden fruit, and told Adam to eat it too.

Adam listened to his wife and at the fruit.

The wife led the husband to sin, and they eventually blamed each other and God for what happened.

Sarai blamed God for her infertility; she rightly recognised God as the One who gives life but she wrongly implied that there was a problem with how God was keeping his promises.

She then devised an alternative plan, a plan she thought of without asking God.

A plan that was, in her mind, better than God’s.

God’s plan has already taken ten years! Sarai’s plan is going to be much quicker.

Like Eve, she then told the husband what to do, except this time, the forbidden fruit is another woman.

And Sarai’s plan started working! Hagar became pregnant!

At long last, Abram will have a son from his own body and Sarai would be the surrogate mother!

However, what Sarai didn’t count on was that Hagar was not just a property but a human with feelings.

And Hagar now finds Sarai contemptible; she looks down on the childless Sarai.

Sarai now blames Abram for the contempt that Hagar is showing her.

She’s blaming Abram for following her plan!

The husband got into trouble for following his wife’s instructions!

So much for happy wife, happy life!

Abram then gave up and said to Sarai in verse 6, “Here, your slave is in your hands; do whatever you want with her.”

Instead of showing leadership and addressed the issue, Abram gave up and let Sarai do whatever she wanted.

He lets wife number one mistreat wife number two, the one who is pregnant with his own child!

It’s really messed up!

And it’s like Egypt happening all over again.

Back in Egypt, Abram didn’t trust God to keep his promises and so he acted on his own ungodly initiative.

This led to a plague in Pharoah’s household as well as almost losing Sarai.

And now, it was Sarai herself who didn’t trust God, who decided to act on her own ungodly initiative rather than waiting on God.

And if we’re honest with ourselves, we are often just like Abram and Sarai

We don’t trust God’s promises nor his timing with his promises.

And so, we try to fix what we think are God’s problems, God’s faults.

We know what obeying God looks like, but we think there is a better way.

We see the problem and the difficulties with God’s way and we think we can do better; we can fix the problems with God’s promises.

But trying to fix what we think as problems with God’s promises is always going to be disastrous.

Eve and Adam thought that they knew better than God; Sarai and Abram thought they knew better than God too and look what happened to them.

It’s like the Dunning-Kruger effect.

We think we know reality better than the God who made it.

It’s narcissistic; it’s foolish and it’s a sin.

When are we tempted to fix God’s promises?

We try to fix God’s promises whenever we water down the good news of Jesus, or whenever we dilute God’s warnings.

The Bible clearly gives us this warning to neglect to “gather together, as some are in the habit of doing.” (Heb 11:25)

But we say, “We don’t have to be at Church, we don’t need to gather together, to be a Christian.”

Where does God say that?

God says that when we’re to outdo one another in showing honour” (Rom 12:1) but we say, “just give your minimum, your spare time, your spare effort, to God and his people.”

Where does the Bible say that?

God’s word is perfect. Don’t try to fix it!

Take God’s word, as it stands, seriously.

Don’t be one of those who are confident in their own understanding that they think they know better than God!

Don’t take ungodly initiatives, even when you’re desperate, thinking that your plan is better than God’s, acting as if you’re the wisest person in the room when in reality you hardly know God at all.

What should we do then, when we’re desperate?

2. Hagar’s found by the God who sees

We need to be found by the God who sees.

Look with me at verse 7, “The angel of the LORD found her by a spring in the wilderness, the spring on the way to Shur.”

Hagar, a foreign slave, ran away from the abuse she was receiving from Sarai.

Along the way, she was met by the Angel of the LORD

What happened here is what’s called a theophany.

Theophany describes the time in the Bible when God appears.

God sometimes appear in the storm, or in a fire, or as the case is here, as a man.

Not every angelic appearance in the Bible is a theophany, but there are times when the interaction between how God shows himself and how people responded that strongly suggests that it was God himself who appeared.

This is what happened in our passage.

In this theophany, God appeared to Hagar on her way to Shur.

Shur was on the border of Egypt; she was about to go back to where she came from.

The Angel of the LORD asks, “Hagar, slave of Sarai, where have you come from and where are you going?”

The Angel of the LORD knew of course, just as God knew when he asked where Adam was in the Garden of Eden, after Adam and Eve sinned against God.

But unlike Adam, Hagar didn’t hide from God but answered truthfully, “I’m running away from my mistress Sarai.”

It makes perfect sense; she was running from Sarai, the one who had been abusing her.

That’s what makes what the Angel of the LORD says next seem shocking, “Go back to your mistress and submit to her authority.”

God tells Hagar to go back to the one who had been abusing and oppressing her.

Why would she go back?

Because God would give her a promise like the promise he gave to Abram.

Verse 11-12, ‘I will greatly multiply your offspring, and they will be too many to count.” The angel of the LORD said to her, “You have conceived and will have a son. You will name him Ishmael, for the LORD has heard your cry of affliction.’

Hagar probably thought no one had heard her cries but God did.

She was not alone when she was crying in her tent after what Sarai had done to her.

God heard her and her son’s name, Ishmael, will be a reminder that God has heard.

Ishmael himself will be a wild donkey of a man; he will be anti-social and people will be anti-Ishmael.

But God promised that many children, countless children, will come through Ishmael.

Hagar then does something extraordinary, something that no one else had done in all the Old Testament.

She gave God a name.

Verse 13, “ So she named the LORD who spoke to her: “You are El-roi,” for she said, “In this place, have I actually seen the one who sees me?”

El-Roi means the God who see me.

God sees Hagar!

Whereas Sarai just sees a womb, and Abram just sees Sarai’s slave, God sees Hagar.

Sarai and Abram saw Hagar and thought about how to take advantage or her.

God saw Hagar and provided for her.

Hagar found out that in all the times when she thought she was alone in her suffering and sorrow, God was watching over her.

She felt invisible but God saw her.

And now, in this place, she had a glimpse of the One who saw her.

And so she named God, El-Roi.

He’s the God who sees her, even in her suffering.

He’s the same God who sees us, even in our suffering.

Today, many women and men suffer in silence, many feel invisible.

Sadly, there are many women suffering in silence, even in our community, even in our country.

Earlier this week, it was reported in the news that one woman has been violently killed in Australia every four days this year.

One woman killed in Australia every four days; that’s unacceptable.

Sisters, my dear sisters, if you feel alone, if you feel like you’re in danger, please talk to someone, please let us know.

We want to help you; we want to support you.

And as much as we try, we know we’re only human, we will have limits.

But not with God, not with El-Roi.

God see us, God hears us; we’re never alone.

God see those who are alone and in suffering.

But do we see them?

So often we’re like Abram and Sarai than we like to admit.

We don’t see, or we refuse to see the suffering of others.

We’re so focussed on our own life, that we fail to see the suffering in other people.

In fact, it’s scary how we often just don’t see people in general.

For example, who did you see when you came to Church this morning?

Do you only see the people who are your friends?

What about those whom you aren’t your friends?

Do you know their names? Do you know anything about them?

May God be merciful to us, that our Church will not be a place where people are content to not see, content to not care about others.

No, may the Lord open our eyes to see with his eyes, so that people will know that they’re seen by us, more importantly, that they are seen by God.

The invisible God see, and every now and then in the Bible, the invisible God is seen.

Hagar got to have a glimpse of El-Roi, the God who sees.

3. Have faith in the One who shines like the sun

Knowing that God see her, Hagar goes back to Sarah, just as the angel of the LORD had told her.

Verse 15, “So Hagar gave birth to Abram’s son, and Abram named his son (whom Hagar bore) Ishmael. 16 Abram was eighty-six years old when Hagar bore Ishmael to him.”

If a family portrait was taken, then it would be Hagar, holding baby Ishmael and Abram looking adoringly into Ishmael’s eyes, as any first time Dad would!

Except this first time Dad was 86 years old!

God is indeed El-Roi, the God who see me.

This is completely different from religions like Theravada Buddhism.

Their goal is to go to higher levels of rebirth to get out of the sufferings of the world.

In Buddhism, when you suffer, you suffer alone.

If you suffer, you bring it on yourself.

From a Buddhist point of view, Hagar brought her trouble on herself.

She deserved it; it was karma.

But God, El-Elroi, saw her, spoke with her, assured her and she was comforted to go back to suffering.

We thank God that the one true God, El-Elroi, is not the God of Hagar or the God of Christians only.

Our God is the God of all.

Our God is the God who knows our suffering.

Jesus Christ, the Son of God, the very representation of God himself, came and suffered.

He willingly came to live among us, to see with eyes like ours what living in a suffering world looks like.

He saw and he suffered with us.

But he didn’t merely come for the experience.

He came to suffer the greatest suffering of all; he suffered the full wrath of God by his death on the cross

He came to suffer, in order to save us from eternal suffering.

He suffered and died so that we might live.

He saw us, but we also saw him!

Open up with me to John 1:18, “No one has ever seen God. The one and only Son, who is himself God and is at the Father’s side —he has revealed him.”

When Jesus came, he came as El-Roi, he came as the God who saw, but at the same time, we also saw him, the image of the invisible God.

In Jesus Christ, we see the God who see us.

We don’t see him perfectly just yet, but by faith, we look forward to seeing him face to face (1 Cor 13:12).

By faith, we look forward to seeing him coming in clouds of heaven (Rev 1:7).

Here’s a glimpse of what Jesus will look like on that day and how we might respond, “He had seven stars in his right hand; a sharp double-edged sword came from his mouth, and his face was shining like the sun at full strength.

Revelation 1:17    When I saw him, I fell at his feet like a dead man. He laid his right hand on me and said, “Don’t be afraid. I am the First and the Last, 18 and the Living One. I was dead, but look—I am alive forever and ever, and I hold the keys of death and Hades.” (Rev 1:16-18)

Jesus will come back to take those who are his, those whom he’s been seeing all this time, those who have suffered for his name, those who denied themselves, carried their cross daily and followed him.

On that day, we will see him in all his glory, brighter than the sun.

On that day, we will see the One who see us.

But until that day, we must keep believing in him, knowing that he sees, even when we feel utterly alone.

In Christ we have a friend who sees us and knows us.

We can always come to him in our times of desperation.

The second verse of ‘What at friend we believe in Jesus’ describes this well:

‘Can we find a friend so faithful, Who will all our sorrows share? Jesus knows our every weakness; Take it to the Lord in prayer.’

Go to the God who see you; that’s what we’re do in prayer.

We go to our Friend, our El-Roi, our Lord Jesus.

And so, what happens when we try to ‘fix’ God’s promises?

Disasters happens.

And when disasters happen, even those disasters we’re responsible for, we can come to the LORD in prayer.

Come to the Lord who sees.

By faith, look forward to seeing him one day, face to face.

Friends, in our desperation, don’t take ungodly initiatives but by faith see the God who sees us.

Remember that the Lord Jesus sees you; He’s El-Roi the God who see me.

How will you live your life knowing that he does see?

It should be a source of comfort for us but also a reason for us to be godly, whether we’re in public or in private.

We can’t hide from him.

What will he find you doing when he comes back?

How will you respond when you see him?

Will we be comforted, knowing with full assurance that you will go home with him because of what He’s done for you?

Or will you be confronted, because you have never put your trust in Jesus?

Friends, Jesus see you now, he see you what’s in your heart.

When he comes, when you see him in his glory, will you be comforted or will you be confronted?

Here are three questions for us to think about this week:

When are we tempted try to ‘fix’ God’s promises?

How does knowing that the Lord Jesus sees you always ought to shape the way we live?

What will Jesus see us doing when he comes back?

Friends, in our desperation, don’t take ungodly initiatives but by faith see the God who sees us.

Let’s pray, “Father, thank you that you are El-Roi, the God who sees us. Please forgive us when we live as you don’t see us. Please forgive us when we foolishly take ungodly initiatives, thinking that we know better than you. Forgive us for our arrogance and our folly. Thank you that despite our arrogance and folly, you still sent your one and only Son to suffer and to die for us. By your Spirit, please open our eyes that we might see what you’re doing in our lives. Please open our eyes to those around us, that we might see them, as you would want us to. And by faith, we look forward to the face when we will behold your glory, when we will see Jesus our Lord face to face. For Jesus’ sake, Amen.”
