Let’s pray:

“Father, please help us to see the hope you give to us through our Lord Jesus even more clearly as we hear from your word today. By your Spirit, fix our eyes and our hearts on Jesus, in whose name we pray, Amen.”

It’s so good to see so many members of our Church are now back at Church regularly after a few months of isolation!

We thank God that the numbers of COVID cases have been kept comparatively low over the past few weeks,

Even as we begin to open again.

While there was a time when it was right to stay at home to prevent the spread of COVID, it was difficult for many of us.

Studies have shown that many Australians, particularly those from 18-24 years old, have experience loneliness while they were socially isolated from one another.

In August this year, 14% of Australians aged from 18-24 years old experienced loneliness most of the time.

16% of this age group experienced loneliness occasionally.

And 26% of this age experienced loneliness some of little of the time.

Social isolation has been linked to mental illness, emotional distress, poor health behaviours, poor sleep, and biological effects, including high blood pressure and poorer immune function.

That’s why so many of us have been looking forward to getting out of our isolation and meet with other people again.

Many of us have had dinners in restaurants, gone back to school, gone back to work, visited our families and many of us have come back to Church.

While there was a time to isolate to prevent the spread of COVID, the time has now come for our community and for our Church to open up again.

Praise God!

While we recognise the danger of what physical isolation can bring, we are often not aware of the danger that spiritual isolation can bring.

Just as physical isolation can lead to long term physical and mental problems, spiritual isolation can lead to long term spiritual problems.

The problem with long term effects of spiritual isolation is that it’s often gone undetected.

Indeed, the person who suffers from it often doesn’t know that he or she has it.

A few weeks ago, a three-year-old boy by the name of AJ Elfalak went missing near his home in the Australian bush.

For three days, hundreds of people were looking for him and thousands of people around Australia would’ve been praying for him.

After three days of search, they found AJ nice and sound, drinking form a creek 200 metres from the place where he was reported missing.

Now we don’t know exactly how AJ got lost, but during those early minutes, he must have thought that everything was OK, that he was just home.

However, those minutes turned into hours and into days.

We’re not sure whether he understood that he was actually lost.

Either way, we’re glad that he was eventually found by the rescuers.

If he stayed in the bush, isolated from his family for much longer, he might not have survived.

Some Christians are like AJ in that they’re spiritually isolated from their Christian family, even though they might not realise it.

Though many people who call themselves Christians might physically live near a Church, they are spiritually isolated from the Church and are spiritually lost.

Even people who physically attend Church can also be lost if they are spiritually isolated from the rest of the Church.

They might be physically present but isolate themselves spiritually from the lives of other Christians at Church.

They don’t speak to other Christians, they don’t attend Growth groups and they don’t read the Bible at home.

They might not notice it at first but they’re going down a slippery slope to spiritual danger.

The Christian writer, CS Lewis puts it like this in his book, the Screwtape Letters:

[T]he safest road to Hell is the gradual one—the gentle slope, soft underfoot, without sudden turnings, without milestones, without signposts.

The way to Hell is an easy slide.

An spiritually isolated person, even one who calls themselves Christians, can slide into Hell without realising it.

We can take this analogy further and apply it to a Church.

When a group of people who call themselves Christians isolate themselves spiritually from the Bible and from the Christian community around them,

They are in danger of sliding down to hell together.

Here’s the first question for this week:

Imagine a Church that is spiritually isolated from other Churches.

They don’t interact with other Churches and have no interests in what other churches do.

What dangers can a spiritually isolated Church fall into?

You can send me your answer by whatsapp, wechat or Zoom or simply by putting your hands up.

What dangers can a spiritually isolated Church fall into?

Talk to the person next to you? You have twenty seconds.

One thing that leads a person or a Church to spiritual isolation is pride.

They don’t think they need other Churches; they don’t think they need any other Christians.

If you think that you’re so spiritually superior to other Christians that you don’t need them, you have already started down the slippery slope.

This brings us back to the Corinthian Church.

Though we don’t know whether they’re spiritually isolated from other churches, they certainly were proud.

They had already shown spiritual decay and the Apostle Paul addressed a number of them throughout his first letter to the Corinthians.

He points the Corinthians to what a spiritually healthy church ought to look like and here at the end, he encourages them not to be spiritually isolated from the other churches.

Here in 1 Corinthians 16, the last chapter of 1 Corinthians, we see three things that will prevent the Corinthian Christians and us from sliding down the slippery slope of spiritual isolation:

Think Globally, gather locally

First, think globally, gather locally.

Paul wants the Corinthians to think globally in terms of their relationship with other churches.

Paul also wants them to think locally in terms of their gathering as a local congregation.

He wants them to think global and think beyond themselves.

He also wants them to think local and not forget about one another.

He wants them to think global and local.

It’s a little bit like a particular type of restaurant that you can find all over the world.

No matter where you’ll go, you’ll find one of these.

Do you know which one?

No, not McDonald’s but Chinese restaurants.

No matter where you go, chances are you’ll find a Chinese restaurant there.

I’ve seen Chinese restaurants around here in Kogarah and even in the small rural towns scattered around Australia.

Even in suburbs where you don’t expect to find Chinese, somehow, you’ll find a Chinese restaurant.

Of course, not all Chinese restaurants are the same; some were set up specifically for Chinese immigrants in mind and others were set up for local Australians in mind.

But no matter where you go, you’ll probably find one in your town somewhere.

Chinese restaurants are global; they’re everywhere.

But they are all local; they’re there to serve Chinese food to the community.

While Chinese restaurants are great to have in the community, it’s even more important to have Christian Churches in our community.

Christian Churches are both global and local too.

There are Christian Churches in many places around the world and we’re there to bring God’s Good News about Jesus to our community.

Our tendency as Churches sometimes tends to be that we’re so focussed on what’s happening inside our Church that we forget what’s happening around us and beyond us.

And so, in this final chapter of 1 Corinthians, Paul wants us to be churches that think globally and to gather locally.

There are three areas in which he wants us to think globally and gather locally.

Firstly, he wants us to think globally and gather locally as we think about how we can support other Churches.

Support other Churches

If you have your Bible there, please open up to 1 Corinthians 16 beginning from verse 1.

“Now about the collection for the saints: Do the same as I instructed the Galatian churches. On the first day of the week, each of you is to set something aside and save in keeping with how he is prospering, so that no collections will need to be made when I come.”

The Church in Jerusalem was facing some economic hardship when Paul was writing this letter and so Paul’s been asking Churches from outside of Jerusalem to give financially to support Jerusalem.

Just as he had asked the Galatian church, Paul’s now asking the Corinthian Church to give to help the Jerusalem Church.

They’re to set aside money to be collected on the first day of the week, that is, Sunday.

Presumably, they collect the money when they gather together for church.

They’re to give in keeping with how God’s prospered them.

They’re not to give money as an afterthought and offer to God whatever they happen to have in their pockets that day.

No, they’re to plan and consider how much God’s prospered them, set the money aside, so that when the collection begins, they’d be ready.

Collecting money at Church for God’s work has been part of the Church service ever since the New Testament times.

And we still do that today in our Church.

In our Church, you can give by placing money in the offertory plate, or you can use the contactless payment, if that suits you better.

For most of us, we give by electronic transfer.

If you would like to know how you can give, please let Range, Michelle or Lynn our wardens know.

They’d love to help you.

Giving to God’s work is what God’s people have been doing since we’ve started gathering as Church.

It’s not compulsory to give.

However, faithful members of the Church, who wants to glorify God, who wants to see the Church grow and who wants to see our community saved, will give.

I thank God for the faithful members of the Church who faithfully gives to support the Church and the ministries here.

That’s what faithful members of the Church do; we give prayerfully and throughfully to support our local Church.

We also think globally and give to support the other churches and other Church workers as the need arises.

Support gospel workers

Look with me at verses 5-6, “ I will come to you after I pass through Macedonia —for I will be traveling through Macedonia— 6 and perhaps I will remain with you or even spend the winter, so that you may send me on my way wherever I go.”

The Apostle Paul doesn’t just want to transmit knowledge to the Corinthian Church.

He could’ve just got them to read the letter, which, as things turn out, will be one of the most important letters ever written because it’s considered by the early Christians to be the very words of God.

Even so, Paul doesn’t just want them to know him through letters.

He wants them to know him.

Paul sees the importance of local ministry.

As important as it is for Paul to think globally and to travel to different countries to spread God’s word and to do God’s work, he also sees the importance of face-to-face personal, local ministries.

He doesn’t want to isolate himself from them; he wants to spend time with them, even the winter with them, God willing.

When he’s ready, he wants them to send him on his way.

However, right now, he must stay at Ephesus for there is a wide door of opportunity for him there.

It won’t be easy, Paul will face many opposition at Ephesus.

There will be many people who doesn’t want Paul to be at Ephesus.

As is true in many cases, gospel effectiveness comes at a high personal cost and Paul is willing to stay and face the oppositions if it means being able to do effective gospel ministries at Ephesus.

Here, Paul sees the importance of the global aspect of the Church.

He will serve where the door of opportunity is wide open for the gospel.

In the meantime, he wants the Corinthian Church to make sure Timothy, one of Paul’s fellow workers, to have nothing to fear when he comes to see them.

Throughout the letter to the Corinthians, we’ve seen that it hasn’t been a particularly welcoming Church, even to their own members.

As someone that Paul has sent, he wants the Corinthians to welcome Timothy and to respect Timothy.

He’s here to do God’s work; they’re not to look down on him.

They’re to respect people who do the Lord’s work.

Here’s where the global and the local aspects of the Church meet.

The local Church is to welcome those from the global Christian community into their midst.

They are to respect these brothers and sisters who have come to the local Church to do the Lord’s work.

And we still do that today in our Church.

When we invite speakers to come to speak with us, we treat them with respect and honour, for they have come to do the Lord’s work.

The speakers we invite don’t want to just give us information; they want to know us and to encourage us.

When they come, it’s an honour for our Church to welcome them in the name of the Lord.

We also support gospel workers, including CMS missionaries, Jairus and his work in the University of Sydney, Nick Hadges and his work in high schools.

We want to support them, not only with money but with prayer and other forms of support.

Support one another

As well as supporting the gospel workers, we must support one another in the local Church.

Look with me at verses 13-14, “Be alert, stand firm in the faith, be courageous, be strong. 14 Do everything in love.”

These are five exhortations that cover a lot of what Paul’s been saying throughout 1 Corinthians.

It’s a little bit like my three questions at the end of the sermon.

There are a summary of what Paul’s been saying.

Be alert

Be aware of where you are spiritually.

Are you isolating yourself spiritually from the rest of the Church?

It’s easy to drift away; it’s easy to slide into spiritual isolation.

It’s easy to be unwelcoming to the Lord’s workers and despise them.

Be alert; think about what you’re saying and think about what you’re doing.

Be alert, lest you sin.

Stand firm in faith – don’t slip on temptations that lead you away from Christ.

Don’t be moved by the oppositions that come because of your faith in Christ.

Stand firm.

Be courageous and be strong; these two go together.

It takes spiritual discipline to follow Jesus.

Christianity is not for cowards.

Jesus is the King; bow to him and no one else.

Do everything in love – that’s the highlight of his discussion on what it means for Christians to gather in worship.

Without love, we are nothing, even if we could achieve great things.

Love is patient and kind; love is how God deals with us.

And so, let’s love one another.

In everything that we do, do it in love.

And we still do that today in our Church.

While we can encourage one another here at church on Sundays, we can also keep encouraging one another in small groups.

We’ve got five Growth groups during the week and two KOG groups on Sundays.

We want to encourage each other to be alert, to stand firm, to be courageous and be strong.

We want to be loving to everyone who comes, especially those who have been serving hard among us.

Last week, I’ve learnt a great tip about giving encouraging feedback.

Before we say one word of criticism, give ten words of encouragement.

While we want people to improve, we also want to do it lovingly.

As the Bible tells us: Do everything in love!

Ten words of encouragement for every work of criticism.

While we want people to improve in their work for the Lord, we also want to be alert lest we act in an unloving way.

And so, think global, gather local.

Remember that the communion of saints is far bigger than the Church we see.

To help us think global and gather local, God provides us with firstfruit leaders.

Following Christ through firstfruits leaders

We follow Christ through firstfruit leaders.

Look with me at verses 15-16, “Brothers and sisters, you know the household of Stephanas: They are the firstfruits of Achaia and have devoted themselves to serving the saints. I urge you 16 also to submit to such people, and to everyone who works and labours with them.

Firstfruits are the fruits that comes first at the beginning of the harvest season.

Their appearance tells us that the fruits are ripe for harvest.

The household of Stephanas are said to be the firstfruits of Achaia, which is the region where the city of Corinth is situated.

By calling them the firstfruits, Paul’s not necessarily saying that they’re the first believers in the region.

However, they are the kind of people that Paul hopes the Corinthian Church would be like one day.

They are the living example of what a Christian household should look like.

We don’t know much about them but we do know two things.

They’re firstly believers of the Lord Jesus.

They also devote themselves to serving the saints.

The Corinthian might have looked for gifted people to be their leaders, people with good oratory skills, people who might demonstrate extraordinary spiritual gifts like speaking in tongues.

But Paul looks for people who are devoted to serving the saints to be leaders of God’s people.

For Paul, a person’s devotion to God and his people is far more important than a person’s skills.

Devotion comes before skills.

Don’t look for clever people to be your leaders at Church.

Look for people who have devoted to serve the saints as leaders in the Church.

These are the people who refresh the spirits of the saints.

Again, we still do that today in our Church.

I would love to have more leaders here at Church.

I would love to have more people taking up responsibilities with the various ministries at Church.

But before I look at their skills, I look to see how devoted they are to serving the saints here at Church.

Do they devote their time and energy to serve the saints here?

Or do they only serve if they must?

A true leader would devote themselves to God’s people, but a mediocre leader would only dispense whatever spare time and resources to God’s people.

I thank God for the leaders that we have at Church.

Many of them have devoted themselves to serving you here.

Many go above and beyond what’s asked of them.

Do pray that God will inspire more people to be firstfruits leaders here at Church, leaders whom we can follow, leaders whom we gladly submit to as they help us follow the Lord Jesus.

If you want to meet these leaders, join a Growth Group, join KOG Youth and KOG Junior.

As we come back together, join the pastoral network activities.

Observe and submit to these firstfruits leaders.

Submit to them as they point us to Jesus, whom we long for.

Long for Jesus

Jesus is whom the firstfruits leaders point us to.

The firstfruits leaders point us to the true firstfruits, the Lord Jesus, who is the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep.

A good firstfruits leader is like a signpost.

They are a little like this new signpost that we got for the mobile pantry.

It’s a massive sign that tells people that the Mobile pantry is on.

There are two massive arrows on both sides, pointing people to where the mobile pantry is.

That’s what good firstfruits leaders do; they point people to Jesus.

Look with me at verse 22, “If anyone does not love the Lord, a curse be on him. Our Lord, come!”

Paul’s focus at the very end of the letter is Jesus.

At the end of the day, it’s all about Jesus.

How do we relate to Jesus?

If we don’t love him, we’ll be cursed; we’ll be damned to a slippery slide to hell.

But for the Christian, we long for Jesus.

We pray that the Lord Jesus will come.

In the original language, the words translated here as our Lord, come, are Maranath a or Maranatha.

The word Maranatha, our Lord come, has often been used throughout the centuries to describe the Christian hope.

We see these words again at the end of the Bible, in the last few verses of the Book of Revelation, “Come Lord Jesus, come.”

In a few weeks, many Churches around the world will have a season called Advent, which is a season to prepare our hearts to remember the first coming of Jesus.

When disasters strike, you often hear Christians pray, “Come Lord Jesus, come.”

We know that the world is dying and we look forward to the coming of Jesus.

Christians look for the coming of Jesus.

That’s why we gather locally on Sundays.

We are preparing ourselves and one another for the coming of Jesus.

We want to help encourage one another not to fall, not to slide, not to spiritually isolate, lest they stop loving Jesus and become cursed.

That’s also why we think globally as Christians.

We want to bring this message to the world.

Jesus is coming; are you ready?

Is your prayer the prayer of the saints, “Come Lord Jesus come”?

And so, what does this mean for us?

Think globally, gather locally under firstfruits leaders as you long for Jesus.

Or you can think of it as the four O’s: Think global, gather local, follow firstfruit leaders, long for Christ.

Think global, gather local, follow firstfruit leaders, long for Christ.

Over the next few Sundays, the global and the local will merge in a number of wonderful ways.

Next week, Bishop Peter Lin will come and speak to us.

A bishop is global in the sense that he oversees a wide area of churches.

But he’s also going to speak to us locally.

He’s going to share the good news of Jesus; make sure you bring your friends and families to come.

We’re then going to have our Thanksgiving service – a celebration of what God has done among us locally during a global pandemic.

We’re then going to have a speaker who will share with us about his ministry to the Jews.

And then we’re going to have the Advent season, preparing ourselves with the upcoming Christmas celebrations.

There are so many ways for us to engage locally and globally over the next few weeks.

Would you pray that in all this, we’ll be faithful signposts, pointing people to Jesus?

Think Global, gather local, follow firstfruit leaders, long for Christ

Here are the three questions for this week:

Am I spiritually isolated from other Christians?

In what way can I contribute to the Church that meets locally here at St Paul’s Kogarah?

Do I pray the prayer of the saints, Come Lord Jesus come?

Friends, as we continue to gather as Church, may God help us to think Global, gather local, follow firstfruit leaders, long for Christ.

Let me finish with the last two verses of the letter of 1 Corinthians:

“The grace of the Lord Jesus be with you. 24 My love be with all of you in Christ Jesus.”

Amen.
