Intro

It’s good to see you all here this morning!

I know that on a day like this, it’s very tempting to stay in bed where it’s nice and warm.

And so, I’m very glad to see you all here.

Sleep is a wonderful thing but it can also be dangerous as well.

Often on our roads, you’ll see a sign that says, Stop, Revive Survive.

It’s a reminder for drivers that if they’re sleepy, they should pullover and stop.

If they dose off while they drive, it can easily, in a matter of seconds, cause deadly accidents.

If we’re not awake when we’re driving, we can put ourselves and others in grave danger.

The same is true for the spiritual things too.

Jesus has warned his disciples over and over again, especially when he was talking about the end of the world, to watch out, to stay alert.

He tells his disciples that there will be people who will actively lie to the disciples, that there will be impressive miracle workers who will try to deceive those chosen by God.

The followers of Jesus are to watch out, to keep an eye out for what’s happening around them, and how they fit in God’s scheme of things.

In today’s passage, in Mark 14:32-72, we see the last hours of freedom that Jesus had before He goes to the cross.

In these precious last hours, he once again warns his disciples again to watch out.

This is a warning that’s very close to Jesus’ heart because he knows that these disciples, as well as every disciple who would in time follow him, needs to stay alert.

This is a warning he wants each Christian, every disciple, to hear.

That’s the question that we’ll need to ask ourselves today: Am I watching, have I been paying attention?

Or have I been sleeping?

In today’s passage, we’ll see three things:

Before we look into it, let’s pray, “Father, by your Spirit, please open our hearts to see your truth clearly, that we may follow Jesus more closely. For Jesus’ sake, Amen.”

Distress in Prayer

If you have your Bible there, please open up with me to Mark 14, beginning from verse 32,

“Then they came to a place named Gethsemane, and he told his disciples, “Sit here while I pray.” 33 He took Peter, James, and John with him, and he began to be deeply distressed and troubled.

When we think about prayer, we usually think of it as a tranquil, reflective activity that we do at Church or at home.

And often, prayers can be very calming.

But in the Gospel of Mark, Jesus is recorded to have prayed explicitly on only three occasions, all of which come at a time of challenge.

The first was in Mark 1:35, when Jesus was challenged to choose between spending more time healing than to preach the gospel.

The second was in Mark 6:46, after Jesus had fed the 5000.

While the occasion for Jesus’ prayer wasn’t recorded in Mark, the actual challenge that Jesus faced was recorded in John 6:14-15, where Jesus was faced with the challenge of being forced to be king or to keep preaching the gospel.

The third occasion is here, in Mark 14, in the Garden of Gethsemane.

Gethsemane is located at the foot of the Mount of Olives, in a valley between the Mount of Olives and Mount Zion.

A few years ago, I went there when I visited Jerusalem and saw many olive trees in the area.

The word Gethsemane means “olive press”.

And here in Mark 15, we see Jesus himself undergo a lot of pressure as he prays at Gethsemane.

Jesus takes three of his disciples with him, Peter, James and John, three disciples who had previously pledged undying loyalty to him.

Jesus explains to them that he’s deeply grieved to the point of death and he asks them to simply remain there and stay awake.

There are no other places in the Bible where Jesus is described to be as distressed as he’s described here.

Even when Jesus is suffering on the cross, there’s hardly any record of Jesus’ distress.

Rather, it’s here, in the garden of Gethsemane, as he prays to God the Father, that Jesus undergoes intense distress.

Indeed, this account here is said to be the most agonizing account in the whole Bible, more painful than the loss of children in the case of King David,

and more heartbreaking than all the Psalms of lament combined.

In another account of the same event, Jesus is said to be sweating drops of blood (Luke 22:44).

Why was Jesus in such distress? Is it because he was afraid of dying?

Though death, particularly death on a cross, would be a horrible thing to go through, Jesus’ distress here is not primarily a distress due to the physical pain that comes with death.

No, Jesus is distressed because he’s about to carry the sin of the world on his shoulders.

He’s about to bear the sin for the sinners of the world.

Jesus himself is sinless, he’s utterly perfect, but he will take on the sin of the whole world.

When he does take on the role as the sin bearer, he will face the full wrath of God for every single sin that he bears.

We all hate it when we see people sinning against us and hurting us.

We hate it when we’re lied to; we hate it when we’ve been hurt.

Now multiply that a billion times, then a billion more, and we’ll begin to see the hatred that God has for all the sins that every human being had committed against him.

That’s what Jesus is facing.

He will face God’s wrath against the liars, he will face God’s wrath against the mass murderers.

He will face God’s wrath against the sins of the world.

Instead of us, Jesus will be the target of God’s wrath against our sins.

It’s because of this wrath that Jesus is distressed to the point of death.

And so Jesus prays, in verse 36, “Abba, Father! All things are possible for you. Take this cup away from me. Nevertheless, not what I will, but what you will.”

There are three parts to Jesus’ prayer.

Firstly, he addresses God as Abba, Father!

The term Abba is an intimate term, a term that a young boy says to his Father.

Like 阿爸 (Abba) in Chinese and Dada in English.

That’s how close Jesus is to God the Father.

Not only is God close to Jesus; he’s also powerful.

Jesus says to his Father, “All things are possible with you”.

God the Father can do all things; nothing is impossible for God.

As Jesus comes to his all-powerful Abba Father, he comes to the second part of his prayer.

Request: “Take this cup away from me.”

The cup here is an imagery used to describe God’s anger towards sin.

It’s the cup of suffering; it’s the cup of God’s wrath.

Jesus is asking God to take the suffering away; he’s asking God to not strike the Shepherd.

He’s asking God to save him from being the suffering servant.

He’s asking God to spare him the cross.

All things are possible with God. Is there another way besides the cross?

This takes us to the final part of Jesus’ prayer,

Relinquish: “Nevertheless, not what I will, but what you will.”

Despite his distress, Jesus chooses to surrender himself, relinquish all that he has and place himself in God’s hands.

He places God’s will above his.

This prayer echoes what he’s taught the disciples earlier, “May your kingdom come, may your will be done on earth as it is in heaven.”

There will be no compromise; God’s will be done.

Jesus’ resolve to do God’s will contrasts that of the disciples’.

Jesus has given them one simple command at the beginning: Remain here and stay awake.

They remained there but they were sleeping.

They couldn’t even stay awake for one hour; it’s not a good sign of things to come.

This happened three times: Jesus goes away praying and when he comes back, he finds them sleeping, three times.

After the third time, he says to them in verse 41, “Are you still sleeping and resting? Enough! The time has come. See, the Son of Man is betrayed into the hands of sinners. 42 Get up; let’s go. See, my betrayer is near.”

After bringing his distress to God in prayer, Jesus overcomes and resolves to do God’s will.

He’s now ready to take the Father’s cup.

Last Monday, I went to a prayer meeting with a number of ministers around Sydney.

It was an encouraging time of getting together and to just pray.

There was a wonderful intensity in the prayers.

It wasn’t just a shopping list of things we want to see from God.

It wasn’t just people mumbling mindless, superficial prayers.

The ministers there were praying according to the concerns of their hearts.

They long to see Jesus glorified in Sydney, they long to see more Gospel workers in the field, they long to see non-believers come to Christ.

You can hear the concern, the struggle and even the distress in some of their prayers.

They wrestle in their prayers.

That’s what Jesus went through for us in Gethsemane.

Jesus prayed with the highest intensity in prayer, sweating blood, being in distress, wrestling against the temptations to leave the path to the cross.

We thank God that Jesus went through that distress, resolved to follow God’s will.

If he didn’t, we’ll still be in the firing line of God’s anger.

But through the distress he experienced in prayer, Jesus prepared himself to receive the Father’s cup.

He’s ready to do the Father’s will, even if it brings about intense suffering.

As he goes on his way to receive the Father’s cup of suffering, he receives nothing by dyspathy from friends and foes.

Dyspathy from friends and foes

Dyspathy is the opposite of empathy.

To show dyspathy to someone is to show hostility to someone.

In Jesus’ case, his friends and foes showed dyspathy in the form of betrayal, injustice and denial.

Firstly, Jesus experiences betrayal.

Look with me at verses 43-44, “While he was still speaking, Judas, one of the Twelve, suddenly arrived. With him was a mob, with swords and clubs, from the chief priests, the scribes, and the elders. 44 His betrayer had given them a signal. “The one I kiss,” he said, “he’s the one; arrest him and take him away under guard.”

Judas, one of the Twelve disciples, betrays Jesus with a kiss.

Usually a kiss shows that you’re a close friend with someone.

However, Judas used a kiss to show his mob which one Jesus is, so that they can take hold of him and arrest him.

Judas had it all planned – he knew where Jesus would be, he had more than enough people to handle any resistance from the other disciples, he would clearly identify Jesus and his mob would capture Jesus before Jesus and the disciples know what was happening.

And it happened just as Judas had planned.

However, long before the plan even entered into Judas’ head, Jesus knew it all along.

Jesus knew who would betray him even before Judas had decided to betray him.

In fact, what Judas had planned was already part of the Holy Scriptures.

Judas didn’t plan a surprise on Jesus; God knew about it all along.

What Judas had planned was only a part of the events that would lead to the Scriptures being fulfilled.

As well as betrayal, Jesus had to face injustice.

Look with me at verses 53-54, “They led Jesus away to the high priest, and all the chief priests, the elders, and the scribes assembled.”

Jesus faces a trial in the High Priest’s house in the presence of the high priest, the chief priests, the elders and the scribes.

When they’re assemble together, they’re called the Sanhedrin.

According to the oral traditions, the Sanhedrin can only meet during daytime if they’re going to discuss a capital offence, they can’t meet on the eve of a feast or Sabbath, and sentencing must take place on the next day (that is, not immediately).

However, this particular meeting of the Sanhedrin has broken all these rules.

They met at night; they met on the eve of the Passover and they passed the sentence on the same day as the hearing!

This particular Sanhedrin had by-passed all the laws in order to do one thing.

Look with me at verse 55, “The chief priests and the whole Sanhedrin were looking for testimony against Jesus to put him to death, but they could not find any.”

They want Jesus dead, and they want to do it quickly.

And so, they broke the laws in order to condemn Jesus.

They have already passed the sentence in their head; they’re just looking for the right charge to condemn Jesus with!

Like Judas, they have planned this well.

They are in complete control over the judges and the witnesses.

However, even with so much control, with so many witnesses giving false testimonies, they still fail to condemn Jesus because their testimonies didn’t agree.

One false testimony seems to have come close.

Someone has said that they heard Jesus said that he can destroy the temple by hand, and he’ll make another in three days.

If they can make this charge stick, then they can charge Jesus with being a threat to public order and possibly have him executed.

However, their testimony on Jesus doesn’t agree even on this.

I don’t know about you, but if someone accuses me of something I didn’t do, I tend to get defensive.

I’ll tell them why they’re wrong to accuse me of something that I didn’t do.

I’ll give them evidence to show that I didn’t do what they’re accusing me of.

But what does Jesus do, he remains silent.

Jesus know that anything that he says in this trial that’s set up to condemn him will twist anything that he has to say.

And so Jesus stays silent.

Even when the High priest asks whether he’s going to answer any of the charges, Jesus stays silent.

However, the High Priest, who’s supposed to be the Judge, becomes an Accuser and asks Jesus, “Are the Messiah, the Son of the Blessed One?”

This is a loaded question and can prove to be a dangerous one for Jesus.

What makes this question a dangerous one for Jesus?

This is a dangerous question because it’s a highly religious as well as a highly political question.

The word Messiah is Christ, which means the anointed one.

The Anointed One in Jewish thought is the one who would rule over God’s people, that is, a King of the Jews.

To claim to be the Messiah or the King of the Jews, would mean treason against the Roman Emperor.

It’s like claiming to be the Prime Minister of Australia when there’s already one there.

It’s also a highly religious question as well because Jesus is claiming to be the Son of the Blessed One, the Son of God.

How does Jesus respond?

Look with me at verse 62, “I am,” said Jesus, “and you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming with the clouds of heaven.”

Jesus doesn’t just say, “Yes I am”, he emphasizes the High Priest’s point by saying that the Son of Man will be vindicated by God himself, coming with clouds of heaven.

Though man condemn him, God will vindicate Jesus in a spectacular way.

As soon as the High Priest heard it, he condemns Jesus as a blasphemer and the whole Sanhedrin agrees and condemns him as deserving of death.

Again, it looks like Jesus is exactly where the Sanhedrin wants him to be.

Jesus is captured, interrogated, condemned, mocked and beaten.

The power dynamics seem to be on the Sanhedrin’s side.

They’re the winners and Jesus the loser.

But once again, Jesus knew it all along that this is going to happen.

He has explicitly said that he’ll be handed over to the chief priests and the scribes and that they will condemn him to death.

The Sanhedrin’s grand plan didn’t surprise Jesus; he knew about it all along.

And so, Jesus’ disciple betrayed him, his enemies condemned him to death and thirdly, another disciple, the Chief of his disciple in fact, denies Him.

As Peter waits for Jesus in the court below, one of the servant girls see Peter and says to him, “You also were with Jesus, the man from Nazareth.”

And Peter responds by saying, “I don’t know or understand what you’re talking about.”

While Jesus is being trialled by the Sanhedrin inside the house, Peter is being trialled outside, by a servant girl.

The difference, of course, is that while Jesus is accused by many false witnesses, Peter is challenged to just speak the truth.

Even as Jesus stands firm in the face of intense opposition, Peter stumbles in the presence of a servant girl.

Peter denies Jesus not just once but three times.

He even starts to curse and swear saying, “I don’t know this man you’re talking about!”

He can’t even bring himself to saying Jesus’ name!

He’s a coward.

Earlier, he slept three times instead of staying awake with Jesus.

Here, he denies Jesus three times.

As soon as denies Jesus the third time, the rooster crowed a second time.

As soon as he hears it, Peter breaks down and weeps.

Why? It’s because he remembers Jesus’ words to him, “Before the rooster crows twice, you will deny me three times.”

Once again, Peter’s denial of Jesus came as a complete surprise to Peter.

He was so sure that he was going to stand up for Jesus, even if it means death.

But he failed Jesus, denied Jesus, exactly as Jesus’ told him.

Again, Jesus knew exactly what was happening.

Though he seemed to be the victim of his circumstances, he was in complete control.

Even as Jesus was betrayed, unjustly condemned and denied, he knows exactly what was happening.

He knew all these things had to happen as he headed toward the bitter cup.

Jesus was determined to do the Father’s will, to drink the bitter cup, to die on the cross for our sins, and if betrayals, injustice and denials be part of the package, so be it.

Earlier this week, my son Ezekiel was supposed to go on a Duke of Edinburgh hike.

But a few weeks ago, he felt a pain in his knees and after speaking with the Doctor, we decided to not let him go.

We’re really glad he didn’t go because it’s been raining hard.

However, if he does go, he’d be given a map, along with various supplies and he’d need to work out how to go from one place to another.

He’d go through slopes, hills and creeks along the way.

The journey will be tough, there will be obstacles to overcome alone the way.

But they’re all part of the package, part of the journey.

If they happen to be on his journey to the destination, then so be it.

For Jesus, his destination, his goal, is to do his Father’s will.

His goal is to go to the cross, take on the fullness of God’s wrath against our sins, in order to save us from our sins.

If betrayals, injustice and denials come along the way, so be it.

On his journey to the cross, the most crucial turning point wasn’t the betrayal or even the trial, as important as they were.

No, the most crucial turning point was Gethsemane.

Jesus felt the most pressure, the most distress in his prayer at Gethsemane.

In his prayer, he had to decide whether it’s his will or the Father’s will.

Once he surrenders himself to the Father’s will, all the other challenges that he meets along the way are simply things that he need to pass through to get to his Father’s will.

Jesus can overcome the betrayal, the injustice and the denial because he’s chosen to surrender himself to do the Father’s will.

In Gethsemane, in the Garden of Tears, in his distress, he’s surrendered himself to do God’s will.

Jesus’ distress in prayer prepares him for the Father’s cup. It’s in light of drinking the Father’s cup that he faces betrayal, injustice and denial along the way.

What does this mean for us?

Don’t doze but pray

Don’t doze but pray that God’s will be done.

Jesus warning to his disciples and to us is this.

Look with me at 14:38. Commit this verse to your memory if you haven’t yet.

“38 Stay awake and pray so that you won’t enter into temptation., The spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak.”

That doesn’t mean you don’t fall asleep physically; we still need to sleep.

But stay awake spiritually, always; always look out to do God’s will.

While humans can’t stay physically awake always, bottlenose dolphins can.

Bottlenose dolphins can’t sleep like humans.

When humans sleep, we have a breathing reflex that keeps us breathing even though we’re unconscious.

But when dolphins are conscious, they stop breathing.

To stay alive, they need to be awake all the time.

So how do they sleep? They sleep with one eye opened.

This means one half of their brain can continue to function and breathe while the other half of the brain rests.

After a while, the two halves of the brain swaps so that the other side can have a break as well.

And so, they’re awake the whole time.

That’s what Jesus’ followers are called to do, they’re to remain awake, spiritually the whole time.

We’re to keep an eye out to do God’s will the whole time.

Left to ourselves, we’ll naturally fall into spiritual drowsiness, we’ll fall into a spiritual sleep.

To be spiritually drowsy means getting lazy about loving God, we become less sensitive to the needs of others.

To be spiritually sleepy means not serving God but serving ourselves first.

And so, Jesus warns us to stay spiritually awake, to be spiritually alert.

Seek God’s will above all things.

And to be spiritually awake, spiritually alert, we must pray.

We must stay awake and pray, lest we fall into temptation.

The spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak.

I feel the battle between the spirit and the flesh, every day. Don’t you?

To help us stay awake spiritually and to pray, we have prayer meetings every Tuesday here at Church and via Zoom.

We have Bible reading and prayer outlines to help us develop a habit of Bible reading and prayer.

Over the past few months, my family have been using that as our guide and it’s been a great help.

The spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak.

It will be a battle, a daily battle.

Not only do we need to battle our own spiritual drowsiness along the way, but there will be external threats as well.

We might face betrayals, injustice and denials as well.

If you’ve been a Christian long enough, changes are you might have already experienced some of these.

But it’s all part of the package, all part of the journey.

If doing God’s will mean facing these difficulties along the way, then so be it.
