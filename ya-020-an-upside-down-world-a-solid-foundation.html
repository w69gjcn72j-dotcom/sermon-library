Young Adults (19–30)

An Upside-Down World, a Solid Foundation

Luke 6:20–49 · Sunday 22 November 2026 · St Paul’s Anglican Church Kogarah · For young adults (19–30) · Sermon by Rev Jerry Zhu

Big Idea: The kingdom of God does not belong to the so-called “self-sufficient”, but to those who know they have nothing and can only depend on God; so build your identity and security on Jesus Christ, and let love, forgiveness, generosity and obedience flow from the grace you have already received.

Aim: by the end of the study, group members will

see that Jesus’ blessings and woes turn the values of our feeds upside down, and name what they are building their identity and security on;

understand that loving enemies, not judging and giving generously flow from who we already are in Christ (identity before behaviour), not from trying harder;

choose one concrete step this week: praying for someone instead of piling on, or one act of generosity, resting on what Christ has already done.

You’ll need: Bibles (CSB), pens, about 60–75 minutes.

1. Connect (10 min)

  • Picture your feed for thirty seconds. What does it say a “blessed” life looks like: the grad job, the overseas trip, the new apartment, the relationship?
  • Call-out culture: have you ever watched someone get piled on online, or been tempted to join in? What made it feel “righteous”?

3. Context

This is Luke’s “Sermon on the Plain”. After a night of prayer and choosing the Twelve, Jesus comes down to a level place and, looking up at his disciples, tells them what following him really means. It parallels the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7), but Luke’s version is sharper and more direct, setting blessings and woes side by side. Jesus is not giving life hacks or “chicken soup for the soul”: he is announcing that the kingdom of God has come, and it turns the world’s values upside down.

4. Discuss

  1. (vv.20–23) Luke tells us Jesus spoke “looking up at his disciples”. Who are these blessings for? Matthew has “poor in spirit”; Luke has “you who are poor”. How do the two fit together?
  2. (vv.20–23) The sermon described a “middle-class spiritual mindset”: God owes me something; I’ve earned it by working hard. Where does that show up for you: marks, a job offer, a ministry role, being “the good one” in your family?
  3. (vv.24–26) Is Jesus cursing the rich? What does “you have received your comfort” mean? The sermon told of a young man filling his heart with comparison on social media. Where do you look for “fullness” that never feels like enough?
  4. (vv.27–30) What do “offer the other also” and “don’t hold back your shirt” mean (see Exodus 22:26–27)? Now read John 18:22–23. How do we tell a heart free of revenge from just letting people walk over us?
  5. (vv.31–36) How is v.31 different from “you’re good to me, so I’ll be good to you”? In v.35, do we become children of the Most High by loving our enemies, or love our enemies because we are his children? Why does the order matter?
  6. (vv.27–28, 37) The sermon’s example: a Christian who prayed for someone being “cancelled” instead of joining the outrage. What is the difference between judging and discerning (John 7:24)? What would it look like in your group chats?
  7. (v.38) Picture the grain seller’s measure: pressed down, shaken together, running over. What does it tell us about God? What does “the measure you use” look like in a share house, a workplace or a friendship that’s gone sour?
  8. (vv.39–49) Why do the two houses look the same until the flood? What is the difference between “trying harder to obey” and building on the righteousness of Christ? If your faith were tested this year (a breakup, a lost job, moving out), what would it be standing on?

Memory verse: “Be merciful, just as your Father also is merciful.” (Luke 6:36)

6. Pray

  • Thank God that his kingdom belongs to the poor, and that our worth lies not in likes or achievements but in the fact that God sees. Confess where we have built on comparison and other people’s praise.
  • Ask for hearts that refuse the cycle of revenge and outrage, and that give generously out of what we have received. Pray for more helpers for KOGKids, KOGYouth and KOGMini, and for accompanists for the Chinese Parallel service.
  • Pray by name for your 3: that they would find their security in Jesus, and for an opportunity to invite one of them to the Christmas Concert.

7. Leader’s Notes

Framing. Joyfully point to Jesus. Guests and not-yet-Christians may be present; never corner anyone. Many young adults live under constant comparison and quick online judgement. The sermon’s key is that identity comes before behaviour: we love, forgive and give not to earn anything, but because we have already received. If someone is “poor, hungry and weeping” right now (a lost job, a relationship, a dream), let them hear v.20 as good news.

Question helps

Q1 — Jesus speaks directly to his followers, with certainty, love and seriousness: “You need to know what following me really means.” No contradiction: the poverty includes both material and spiritual poverty. Those with nothing to lean on often find it easiest to admit they need God; to be poor in spirit is to see we cannot save ourselves, and to receive God’s free grace.

Q2 — The sermon’s example: a Christian manager made redundant in the shift to AI, who saw that his “riches” were a mindset, “I am enough on my own.” For young adults the same mindset can attach to marks, career, or being the reliable one.

Q3 — No: Abraham, Job and Nicodemus were wealthy. The woe is on those who make wealth their comfort, ultimate security and identity. Comparison promises fullness, but it is false and fleeting; what the young man needed was to admit the hunger of his soul and come to Christ. The sermon also told of a sister who needed praise for her service (v.26): our worth lies in the fact that God sees.

Q4 — The coat was a night blanket that had to be returned by sunset; it stands for livelihood and dignity, the cheek for reputation. Jesus calls us out of the cycle of revenge, entrusting both to the Father as Judge and provider. But at his trial Jesus did not turn the other cheek (John 18:22–23): this is a posture of the heart, not a rigid rule. With ongoing violence, crime or protecting the innocent, seeking justice and setting boundaries fit Scripture.

Q5 — The world: first receive, then give. Jesus: give first, expect nothing back. We don’t become God’s children by loving enemies; we already are his children in Christ, so we can live out his likeness, gracious to the ungrateful and evil (Romans 5:8). Reverse the order and it becomes moralism.

Q6 — Praying for someone isn’t approving their mistake; it is refusing to join the cycle of hatred. Christians must discern right from wrong, but Jesus forbids self-righteous, condemning judgement, which forgets that we too are sinners saved by grace. The opposite of judging is not “no standards” but humble forgiveness.

Q7 — The generous seller heaps the grain until it spills over: the buyer receives more than he deserves, as God gives us far beyond what we deserve. “The measure” is not karma but a law of relationships: the stingy live in scarcity, the generous in abundance. Sermon example: the brother at work who held grudges and lived in a world where everyone was out to get him.

Q8 — The foundation is out of sight until the test. Jesus asks not for perfect obedience but for loyalty: if he is Lord, life heads towards doing. The foundation is Christ’s righteousness, not our performance. Sermon example: the brother who couldn’t forgive until his foundation shifted from “I must do it” to “Christ has already done it”.
