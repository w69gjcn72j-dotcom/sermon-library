Young Adults (19–30)

Come to the Wedding of the King's Son

Matthew 22:1–14 · Sunday 6 September 2026 · St Paul's Anglican Church Kogarah · For young adults (19–30) · Preached by Rev David Yung

Big Idea: Don't insult the Heavenly Father's invitation with excuses or defiance — come when he summons, clothed in what he provides.

Aim: (1) See the gospel as an invitation into the Father's joy in his Son, not a performance review. (2) Name the good, defensible things — work, study, side projects, the group chat — that quietly outrank God's summons. (3) Take one step in the 3 · 2 · 1 · 0 rhythm this week, and consider one place to serve at St Paul's.

You'll need: Bibles (CSB), pens, about 60–75 minutes.

Connect (10 min)

  • What's the most awkward invitation you've ever had to turn down — and what excuse did you actually give versus the real reason?
  • Scroll back through your camera roll to last week. If a stranger judged your priorities from those photos alone, what would they conclude?

Read — Matthew 22:1–14 (CSB)

1 Once more Jesus spoke to them in parables: 2 "The kingdom of heaven is like a king who gave a wedding banquet for his son. 3 He sent his servants to summon those invited to the banquet, but they didn't want to come.

4 Again, he sent out other servants and said, 'Tell those who are invited: See, I've prepared my dinner; my oxen and fattened cattle have been slaughtered, and everything is ready. Come to the wedding banquet.'

5 But they paid no attention and went away, one to his own farm, another to his business, 6 while the rest seized his servants, mistreated them, and killed them. 7 The king was enraged, and he sent out his troops, killed those murderers, and burned down their city.

8 Then he told his servants, 'The banquet is ready, but those who were invited were not worthy. 9 Go then to where the roads exit the city and invite everyone you find to the banquet.' 10 So those servants went out on the roads and gathered everyone they found, both evil and good. The wedding banquet was filled with guests.

11 When the king came in to view the guests, he saw a man there who was not dressed for a wedding. 12 So he said to him, 'Friend, how did you get in here without wedding clothes?' The man was speechless.

13 Then the king told the attendants, 'Tie him up hand and foot, and throw him into the outer darkness, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth.'

14 For many are invited, but few are chosen."

Context

This is the final week before the cross. Jesus is in the temple courts and the religious leaders have just decided to arrest him. He tells three judgment parables back to back, each sharper than the last, and this is the third. In that world a king's invitation was not something you could leave on read — it was a summons, and declining after you had accepted was a public act of disloyalty.

Discuss

  1. Read verses 1–4. What has the king already finished before anyone is asked, and what do the guests contribute? How does that cut against the "prove yourself" instinct most of us carry into work and study?
  2. Verse 3: the king follows up people who had already said yes. What does that show about God's posture towards people who have drifted?
  3. Verse 5: a farm and a business. In your world, what are the modern equivalents — the good, defensible commitments that no one could criticise you for? Why are those the effective ones?
  4. Verses 6–7: indifference turns into hostility. Have you seen that pattern — online or in yourself — where a shrug about Jesus hardens into something sharper? What drives it?
  5. Verses 8–10: the servants gather "both evil and good." We live in a culture that sorts people fast — who's in, who's cancelled, who's worth the effort. What does the King's non-criterion do to that instinct?
  6. Verses 11–13: he got in free and was still put out. What was actually wrong with him? Why does it matter that he was already seated inside the feast?
  7. The king calls him "friend" and asks a question rather than simply throwing him out. What does the man's silence say — and what does the question say about the King?
  8. Verse 14. Where is the honest evidence that you have come to the marriage rather than curating a good Christian profile — in your calendar, your money, your friendships, your Sundays?

Live It Out

Across our whole church we're living one simple rhythm — 3 · 2 · 1 · 0. This passage is why: the servants were sent to invite, not to filter.

  • PRAY FOR 3 — three people who aren't Christians yet. Housemates, the person two desks over, someone from your degree. Names, not categories.
  • CONNECT WITH 2 — a real conversation with two of them this week. Meals and walks, not links and reels.
  • INVITE 1 — ask one of them to something at St Paul's. You're not recruiting; the feast is already paid for.
  • 0 LEFT UNLOVED — on Sunday, find someone new or on their own and stay with them past the awkward part.

This week's challenge: Two things. First, open your calendar and move one good thing that has been outranking God's people and God's word. Second, put your hand up somewhere: we need assistants for KOGKids, KOGMini and KOGYouth, and four new accompanists for the Chinese Parallel congregation. Message one of the ministry team this week.

Memory verse: "Tell those who are invited: See, I've prepared my dinner; my oxen and fattened cattle have been slaughtered, and everything is ready. Come to the wedding banquet." — Matthew 22:4 (CSB)

Pray

  • Thank Jesus that the feast is his and that you are invited into the Father's delight in him — not asked to earn a seat.
  • Pray by name for your three friends who aren't Christians yet: that God's kindness would lead them to repentance, and that he would open one door for you this week.
  • Pray for the helpers and accompanists we're asking God for, and that this group would be a place where people are gladly clothed in Christ rather than performing for one another.

Leader's Notes

Keep this joyful. The parable ends in judgment, but its engine is a Father's love for his Son, and the invitation stays open all the way to verse 12.

This age group will include people who grew up in church and quietly stopped coming, and people who have never come at all. Both are welcome; neither should be put on the spot. Let them answer the King in private.

Wrong turn 1 — decoding the wedding robe. Groups want a neat allegory; Jesus gives none. What he shows is the man's refusal: clothing was available and he could not be bothered. Preach the refusal, not the symbol.

Wrong turn 2 — letting v.14 become a predestination argument or an anxiety spiral. Matthew's "called" is the wide outward invitation; the chosen are known by an obedient response. The verse is meant to move you to come today.

Question helps

  • Q1 — Everything is ready (v.4); the guests bring only themselves. That is the direct opposite of the performance economy they live in. Joseph Hart: "All the fitness he requireth is to feel your need of him."
  • Q2 — He chases. These people had already said yes, and he sends again rather than sulking — useful for anyone who has drifted and assumes the door has closed.
  • Q3 — Expect: the shift, the side hustle, the gym block, the thesis, the weekend away. None wrong — which is the point. Apathy never feels like rebellion; it feels like a full calendar.
  • Q4 — Withdrawing from an accepted royal invitation was withdrawing loyalty. Watch the trajectory: paying no attention (v.5) becomes seizing and killing (v.6).
  • Q5 — "As many as you find" — evil and good alike, no criterion at all (cf. Acts 13:46). A real challenge to call-out instincts and curated friendship.
  • Q6 — Contempt, not poverty; and he was already inside. Judas was in the group, at the supper, trusted with the money, and lost — sobering for anyone whose faith is mostly inherited.
  • Q7 — "Friend" is a genuine appeal. His silence is his own verdict; he has no answer because there is none.
  • Q8 — Steer towards evidence rather than feeling: diary, money, tongue, Sunday, service.
