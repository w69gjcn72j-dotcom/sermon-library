Race, Gender, Class — Think Like a Son, Not a Slave

Galatians 3:27–4:7 · Sunday 13 September 2026 · St Paul's Anglican Church Kogarah · KOGYouth · High School · Leader's Copy

Big Idea You are baptised into Christ, clothed with him, one in him. So don’t identify yourself as a slave would, but as God does: his adopted son.

Aim

Students can name the three markers the ancient world used — race, class and gender — and say why being in Christ outranks all three.

Students can explain in their own words what it means to be “clothed with Christ” and adopted as God’s son.

Each student names one person at school they have quietly sorted into a box, and one step they will take this week to treat them as someone Christ died for.

You'll need Bibles (CSB), pens, whiteboard or butcher’s paper, ~60–75 minutes

Connect (10 min)

  • You meet someone new at school and they ask, “So who are you?” You get five words. What are they? Now the harder question: who decided those five words were the important ones — you, or everyone else?
  • Every school has labels that everybody knows without anybody saying them out loud. What happens to someone who doesn’t fit the label they’ve been handed? Have you ever been given a label you didn’t choose?

Read — Galatians 3:27–4:7 (CSB)

27 For those of you who were baptised into Christ have been clothed with Christ. 28 There is no Jew or Greek, slave or free, male and female; since you are all one in Christ Jesus. 29 And if you belong to Christ, then you are Abraham’s seed, heirs according to the promise.

4:1 Now I say that as long as the heir is a child, he differs in no way from a slave, though he is the owner of everything. 2 Instead, he is under guardians and trustees until the time set by his father. 3 In the same way we also, when we were children, were in slavery under the elements of the world.

4 When the time came to completion, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law, 5 to redeem those under the law, so that we might receive adoption as sons. 6 And because you are sons, God sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, “Abba, Father!” 7 So you are no longer a slave but a son, and if a son, then God has made you an heir.

Context

Paul is writing to churches with Jewish Christians and Greek Christians in the same room, and they are pulling apart over who is really in. In the ancient world three things sorted everybody: your race, whether you were slave or free, and whether you were male or female. A Jewish man’s morning prayer actually thanked God that he wasn’t a Gentile, wasn’t a slave, and wasn’t a woman. One group looking down on another was completely normal. Paul says that for anyone baptised into Christ, those three markers no longer decide who you are.

Discuss

  1. What happens to a person who is baptised into Christ? What picture does Paul use — and what does that picture suggest about where the new identity comes from?
  2. List the three pairs Paul names. Why do you think he picked those three and not, say, rich and poor or young and old?
  3. Paul says “you are all one in Christ Jesus.” Does that mean you stop being Chinese, or Australian, or a girl, or a guy? If not, what has actually changed?
  4. If you belong to Christ, what does Paul say you become? What is an heir, and what is an heir waiting for?
  5. Paul says a young heir “differs in no way from a slave.” How can someone own everything and still live like a slave? What is he waiting for?
  6. What did God do “when the time came to completion”? Notice the two things said about the Son — born of a woman, born under the law. Why does each one matter?
  7. Why does it matter that God sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, and not just a set of instructions? What do we get to call God, and what does that name tell you about the relationship?
  8. “No longer a slave but a son.” Where at school do you still think like a slave — sorting other people, or yourself, by race, money or gender? What would thinking like a son look like on Monday morning?

Live It Out

KOG 3·2·1·0 — pray for 3, connect with 2, invite 1, 0 left unloved.

  • Pray for 3 — name three friends who aren’t Christians yet. Write them down. Pray for them by name every day this week.
  • Connect with 2 — have a real conversation with two of them. Not a gospel presentation; a conversation. Ask about their week and actually listen.
  • Invite 1 — ask one of them to something: KOGYouth, church, youth group games night.
  • 0 left unloved — look around your growth group and your year group. Who is on the outside? Go to them.

This week's challenge: This week, sit with or start a conversation with one person at school you would normally walk straight past because they’re “not your group”. Notice what you assumed about them before you spoke, and what turned out to be true.

Memory verse: “So you are no longer a slave but a son, and if a son, then God has made you an heir.” — Galatians 4:7 (CSB)

Pray

  • Thank God for sending his Son to redeem us and adopt us — that our identity is settled by him and not by anybody’s opinion of us.
  • Pray by name for the three friends on your list — that God’s kindness would lead them to repentance, and that he would give you a chance to point one of them to Jesus.
  • Ask God to show you where you have been sorting people by race, money or gender, and to make KOGYouth a group where nobody is left on the outside.

Leader's Notes

Keep this warm and joyful. Some of your students are not yet Christians, and this passage is good news for them — not a test they’ll fail. The whole point is that identity is given, not earned. Never corner a student over their answers about race or gender; questions 2, 3 and 8 can get personal fast, so let students pass, and be ready to share your own honest example first. If a student says something clumsy about another group, don’t shame them — take the sermon’s line: “before we point the finger, let’s have a good look at ourselves.”

Two likely wrong turns

  • Wrong turn 1 — “so differences don’t matter at all.” Paul does not say we stop being Jew or Greek, male or female. He says those are no longer the most important markers. Christ is. If a student concludes the Bible flattens everyone into sameness, walk them back to the sermon’s line: we are still all those things, but they are no longer what identifies us.
  • Wrong turn 2 — “son” as something you earn by trying harder. Adoption is entirely God’s act in verses 4–5; the Spirit in verse 6 is given because you are sons, not to make you one. The response is to live out an identity already given, not to work up an identity you hope to get.

Question helps

  • Q1 — Baptised into Christ, and clothed with Christ. The picture is clothing: like a beggar invited into a family’s home and given new clothes, or a stranger brought into a wedding and handed wedding clothes. The clothes are given, not bought. From the moment you believed, you are wrapped up in Christ (compare Galatians 2:20).
  • Q2 — Jew or Greek (race), slave or free (class), male and female (gender). These are the three markers that categorised the ancient world and shaped how society identified you. We still use them; we just use nicer words.
  • Q3 — No. We are still Chinese or Australian, still male or female. What changes is the ranking. Christ is the identity that now outranks the rest — so I identify with you not because you speak Chinese or English, but because we are both in Christ.
  • Q4 — Abraham’s seed, heirs according to the promise. An heir is someone waiting on everything the Father has promised. We are saved by God’s One and Only Son, and then adopted as sons.
  • Q5 — The heir technically owns everything belonging to his father, but he is too young, so the father sets a time. Until then he lives under guardians and trustees, treated like any slave. Paul says that was God’s people before Jesus came, “in slavery under the elements of the world” — the rules and common sense of a culture that shape how we see everything, functioning like slave masters. For Israel it was even God’s own law: “the law, then, was our guardian until Christ” (3:24). The law restrains our rebellion, but restraint is not transformation.
  • Q6 — God sent his Son, to redeem those under the law, so that we might receive adoption as sons. Born of a woman: he shared our humanity. Born under the law: he lived under the law’s scrutiny and, unlike us, lived a perfectly righteous life under it, so the law could not convict him — and then willingly took the law’s conviction for everyone who trusts him. That was the price.
  • Q7 — Because God doesn’t look at us from a distance. All three persons of the Trinity are at work in verses 4–6 so that we can actually speak to the Father. We get to cry “Abba, Father” — the word a child uses at home. Paul deliberately puts a Hebrew word beside a Greek one so that everybody in that bilingual church could hear it in their heart language.
  • Q8 — Answers will vary; press for the specific. The sermon’s test is division: one sign we still think like slaves is when we divide ourselves by culture, class or gender. Thinking like a son looks like asking of the person in front of you, “how can I help this person walk toward Jesus, not away from him?”

Our church vision

This is a passage about a church “from faith to faith” — one church on God’s mission, not a set of groups sorted by accent. That is why we work so hard at translation in our combined congregation, and why our Chinese-speaking and English-speaking brothers and sisters cry out to the one Father, each in their own tongue: Abba, Father, 天父. Our 90-day goal of six additional qualified assistants for KOGKids, KOGMini and KOGYouth is a direct outworking of this study — some of your students are exactly who God is calling. Ask them. And our 3·2·1·0 rhythm is how the “0 left unloved” of verse 28 actually happens at school this week, as we press on toward 75 households serving densely at St Paul’s (currently 50).
