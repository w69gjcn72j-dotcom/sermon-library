Jesus our Peace

Passage: Ephesians 2:11-15

Big Idea: Christ is our peace, who by his blood brought those who were once far away near, tore down the wall of hostility and created in himself one new humanity

1. Foreigners without hope

2. The violent act that brings peace

3. Christ our peace creates new humanity

In about six weeks, Australia will be asked to vote again, not for a new government, but on a referendum about a new law to change the Constitution.

It’s a change to establish a new committee called an Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Voice.

We can choose either Yes or No.

I won’t go into the details here except to say that there are very strong opinions about it in both the Yes camp and the No camp.

On the one hand those in the Yes camp say that those in the “No” are leaving the gap bewtween indigenous Australians and non-indigenous Australians incredibly high.

To say “No” means leaving keep a division wide open between one group of Australians and another.

On the other hand, those in the No camp say that those in the “Yes” camp are themselves causing a deep divide in the Australian community.

To say “Yes” will introduce a permanent change in the Constitution of Australia based on a person’s race.

Both camps are saying that the other camp is divisive.

Sadly, there had been incidences where both sides have been quite hostile to one another.

Both sides want peace in our society, but they have very ways to approach it.

Of course, it’s not the first time that people have different opinions of how we can bring peace about.

People throughout history and across cultures have very different approaches of how they bring peace about.

Some do it through diplomacy; others do it through acts of violence.

How does God bring peace?

The Bible tells us that God brings peace about through violence.

To be precise, God brings peace about through one act of violence.

How does God bring peace through one, single act of violence?

Look with me at Ephesians 2, beginning from verse 11.

In today’s passage, we’ll see three things:

1. Foreigners without hope

2. The violent act that brings peace

3. Christ our peace creates new humanity

Before we begin, let’s pray:

“Our God, our heavenly Father, by your Holy Spirit, please open the eyes of our hearts that we might see the glorious Lord Jesus even more clearly, in whose name we pray, Amen.”

1. Foreigners without hope

Look with me at Ephesians 2:11-12, ‘So, then, remember that at one time you were Gentiles in the flesh—called “the uncircumcised” by those called “the circumcised,” which is done in the flesh by human hands. 12 At that time you were without Christ, excluded from the citizenship of Israel, and foreigners to the covenants of promise, without hope and without God in the world.’

Gentiles are people who aren’t Jews.

In the Old Testament of the Bible, which Jews believe as their Scripture, God has promised to bless the descendants of Abraham.

The male descendants of Abraham would be circumcised to show that they’re God’s people, God’s chosen ones.

And so, the Jews would see the Gentiles, the non-Jews, as the uncircumcised.

These uncircumcised Gentiles were not part of God’s people.

The Jews had the hope of Christ, that is, God’s anointed King, ruling over them and for them.

They believed the Christ will rule with perfect justice and overthrow any oppression that they as God’s people were facing.

The Gentiles, on the other hand, have no such hope.

By the way, if you’re not a Jew, this is talking about you; it’s talking about me.

We, the non-Jews, are without Christ, excluded from the citizenship of Israel, and foreigners to the covenants of promise.

Many of us here would’ve been foreigners in Australia at some stage.

If you’re not a citizen or a Permanent Resident of Australia, there is always a certain degree of uncertainty.

You can stay here only as long as your visa allows, you can’t get Medicare, and you can’t work in any job you choose.

However, if you’re a citizen of Australia, you don’t need to fear of getting kicked out, you have access to the benefits of Medicare and you have a lot of job options to choose from!

It’s great to be an Australian citizen!

As Gentiles, we’re like foreigners with no PR, no citizenship.

We have no hope of any promises of God.

All the blessings and all the promises of peace and life that God gave to his own people are off-limits to us.

We’re without hope and without God in the world.

But we do hope as Gentiles, don’t we?

We hope for a better life.

For many fathers and mothers among us, we hope for our children.

This is especially the case for many immigrants, who move to Australia in order to give their children a better hope, and a better life.

And don’t Gentiles have gods?

Gentiles have all kinds of gods, all kinds of religions.

This was true back in Paul’s days and is also true in our days.

Throughout history, Gentiles have many gods to choose from to worship.

And so, what does the Bible mean when it says that we Gentiles have no hope and no God?

As Gentiles, we might have hope, but not a hope that lasts.

We live in an ever-changing world.

Anyone who puts his hope in the material world must remember that any material wealth can quickly disappear.

Nothing in the material world lasts.

And though we as Gentiles might have many gods to choose from, the fact remaisn that we don’t have access to the one, true God.

If, as the Bible tells us, there is one true God, then picking any god that we choose is an act of disobedience.

In Chines culture, there’s a saying that describes a cowardly, disobedient son, “Recognising thief as father”.

When we choose our own god, rather than obeying the one true God, then we too, are sons of cowardice, “Recognising thief as father”.

When we worship a god of our choosing, we worship a false god, we recognise a false father.

As Gentiles, we’re without Christ, and so we’re without hope and without God.

2. The violent act that brings peace

However, God the Father didn’t want us to be without him or without hope. That’s why he sent His Son Jesus Christ.

Look with me at Ephesians 2:13, “But now in Christ Jesus, you who were far away have been brought near by the blood of Christ.”

You were without hope and without God, BUT…

That’s what we see in the gospel of Jesus: We were in a hopeless situation BUT God does something about it.

Though we were once far off, God brought us near.

How? By a violent act.

God brought us near by the death of his Son, by the Blood of Christ.

Throughout history, we human beings have always wanted peace, as long as it’s on our terms.

The Roman Emperor Hadrian is said to have sought "peace through strength or, failing that, peace through threat.”

Alexander Hamilton, one of the founding fathers of the united States, also argued for peace through strength by building strong garrisons in the west and a navy in the east.

Both men wanted peace, and they, like many men and women throughout history, were willing to commit acts of violence to make it happen.

How does God bring peace?

God brought peace not by bringing violence on his enemies but by bringing violence on his Son, in order to save us, his enemies.

God the Father sent Jesus to the cross, a place of shame, a place of death, in order to die for foreigners, strangers, like us, to bring us peace.

What kind of God is this?

Doesn’t God the Father love God the Son?

Why has God the Father committed such violence against His Son, His One and Only Son?

It’s because he loves us; that’s how much God the Father loves us!

And that’s how much Jesus himself loves us as well!

Jesus loves God the Father and obeys Him; he was willing to go and die a violent death on the cross.

The cross was a horrible execution.

It was so bad that Cicero, the Roman statesman, said that the word cross should never be mentioned in polite society.

It was shameful also to the Jews as well, for everyone who is hung on a tree was cursed, which was what happened when Jesus died on the cross.

Even so, Jesus went to the cross because he loved us, as a shepherd would his own sheep.

As Jesus himself had said, ““I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.” John 10:11

Jesus went to the cross because he loved the Father.

Jesus also went to the cross because he loved us.

By his blood, by his violent death, we were brought near.

Look with me at verse 14, “For he is our peace, who made both groups one and tore down the dividing wall of hostility.”

Jesus is our peace; let that sink in.

Jesus is our peace.

It’s by his blood that he brought us peace. He is our peace.

Jesus’ death was violent, but even more than that, his death itself did something violent.

Jesus’ death tore down the dividing wall of hostility; the death of Jesus tore down our hostility to one another.

To have true peace, you don’t keep building up your arsenal, in order to be a threat to others, or worse, to actually commit acts of violence to others.

To have true peace, you need God’s one act of violence, the death of Christ, which brings about the destruction of what divides us.

It’s by the blood of Jesus, that the walls of hostility are torn down.

It’s only when these walls of hostility are torn down that we have true peace.

On Thursday, I was at the Asian Ministry Conference where our Archbishop Kanishka Raffel was sharing the Bible with us.

In the Archbishop’s talk, he mentioned that the ancient city of Antioch was a very crowded city, with people from all corners of the Roman empire.

It was a melting pot of people, with Greeks and Jews, Syrians, Gauls, Germans and Barbarians.

As with most cities where you have many people with very different cultures between them, the city of Antioch became a place of urban unrest.

In order to keep the peace, the city was divided into eighteen different quarters, with each quarter separated from one another by big, tall walls.

However, when the message of Jesus reached the city, the Christians were crossing these walls to meet one another.

For the Greek Christians were not Greek first but Christians first.

The Jewish Christians identify themselves as Christians before they identify themselves as Jews.

Something extraordinary happened to this people.

Their most important identity is no longer their culture; their most important identity is now who they are in Christ.

And the same goes for today.

As Chinese Christians we don’t see ourselves first as Chinese, but as those belong to Christ.

As Australian Christians, we don’t see ourselves first as citizens of Australia but as those who belong to Christ.

It’s only when we recognise the significance of the death of Jesus Christ that we can have true peace among us.

3. Christ our peace creates new humanity

Look with me from the end of verse 14, “In his flesh, 15 he made of no effect the law consisting of commands and expressed in regulations, so that he might create in himself one new man from the two, resulting in peace.”

In the laws of God in the Old Testament, God tells his people how they are to be different from from the others.

There were rituals to follow, and moral codes to abide with.

The law tells you what it means to live as God’s people.

It tells you how to love God, and how to have a proper relationship with God.

But now, by his death on the cross, Jesus made the law of no effect; he nullified the law.

Following the law of the Old Testament is no longer what it means to live as God’s people.

Following the law of God in the Old Testament is no longer how you are to relate to God.

From now on, you relate to God by how you relate to Jesus.

If you are in Jesus, you are in a right relationship with God.

If you’re without Jesus, then no matter how hard and how well you keep the law, whether you’re a Jew or a Gentile, you won’t be in a right relationship with God. Being a Christian is not becoming a Jew, by following the law.

It’s far more than that; it’s becoming part of the new humanity.’

I still remember my parents’ citizenship ceremony, where I was naturalised to be an Australian citizen as well.

After becoming citizens, we are no longer visitors to Australia.

We belong here. Hong Kong is not my home anymore, Australia is.

I missed Hong Kong and would love to travel back and visit there, but Australia is now my home.

When we become Christians, we don’t just receive a new identity document like a citizenship paper or a new passport.

There is fundamental change in who we are.

We’re now a new creation.

This goes deeper than our skin colour; it reaches far deeper than our culture; it’s even more fundamental than our DNA.

We are a new creation.

It’s as if we’ve died and now given a new life.

This is what the Bible says in Romans 6:8-9, “Now if we died with Christ, we believe that we will also live with him, 9 because we know that Christ, having been raised from the dead, will not die again. Death no longer rules over him.”

Christ died and rose again.

If we’re in Christ, we too, have died with him and we have risen with him, spiritually speaking.

And one day, we’ll rise with him in a new body.

That’s the hope that we now have.

We used to have no hope, no God.

But now in Christ, who by his death had won peace for us, we have hope of eternal life.

We have real hope because we believe in Jesus, the real God.

Last Monday, we held Yimin’s funeral here.

It was a sad occasion, but it’s also a time to remember the hope that Yimin has in Jesus Christ.

Though she struggled with a weak body, she will rise with a new body, a new body that will not die.

What a marvellous hope Yimin has in Christ!

It’s a hope that Jesus offers to all those who are his.

Christ’s death brings true peace for it brings true hope.

Christ’s death tears down the wall of hostility between us.

That’s what we see here on Sunday mornings here at Church.

In our world, we see the hostility between the old and the young, the hostility between one culture and another.

The walls of hostility are as tall as they’ve been in our world.

But not so in the Church.

Here the old sit next to the young, those from one culture or language group sits next to someone from another culture or speaks another language.

We’re not perfect yet, far from it! But we’re learning.

Whenever we feel hostile towards another culture, we think about the violence God brought about to bring peace.

Whenever we feel superior to another group, whenever we look down on another group that lacks education, that lacks money, may God be merciful and forgive us.

May God help us to remember who these people are, people for whom Christ died, people for whom God sent Jesus to the cross.

And so, friends, God has committed one act of violence, a terrible and yet the most loving act of violence, to bring us peace.

He sent Jesus to the cross, and by his blood, he tore down the wall of hostility, brought us who were far to himself and to all those whom His Son saved.

By his death, Jesus made for himself a new humanity.

By his blood, we have peace.

Since we have peace by his blood,

Don’t be hostile any longer, for Jesus is our peace

Some of you would’ve heard me refer to this quote by Rev Michael Duckett before.

Michael is an indigenous minister who looks after the aboriginal people in our community.

He’s seen the devastations that Australians, especially white men, have brought to his community.

And yet, listen to what he says, “I’ve seen men who hate white people – who’ve been traumatised and abused by them their whole lives – but when God comes into their lives, their hearts are changed.

They know, ‘This is my brother now’, and they’ll sit with them. Only God can change the heart of a person... to love their neighbour as God has said.”

Is there someone you are you tempted to be hostile to?

What hostility must you overcome in light of what God has done for you in Christ? Sometimes it’s very hard to be loving to those you’re hostile to.

May God be merciful and help us overcome our hostility towards others, towards the foreigners, towards people who are different than us, in light of the cross of Christ.

Instead, may we bring the peace of God to those who had only known hostility all their lives, that they too, might be brought near, that they too might know Christ as their peace.

Don’t be hostile any longer, for Jesus is our peace

Here are the three questions for this week:

1. Do you know someone who’s without Christ and so without hope and without God?

2. How does the blood of Christ help you understand how much God wants you to have his peace in Christ?

3. What acts of hostility must you let go of in light of the blood of Christ?

Don’t be hostile any longer, for Jesus is our peace.

Let’s pray, “Our Heavenly Father, we thank you for the love you've shown us in our Lord Jesus Christ. Thank you that out of your love for us, you sent Jesus your only Son to die for us on the cross. Father, please forgive our hostility to you and our hostility to one another. Help us to always remember the peace that Christ’s blood has won for us, that though we were one far off we’re brought near, those once we were your enemies Yung made us your sons and daughters. Let us actively and internally live in the peace of Christ, Christ who is himself our peace, in whose name we pray, Amen “
