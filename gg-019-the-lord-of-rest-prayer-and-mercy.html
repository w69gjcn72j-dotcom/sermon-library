Home Groups (adults)

The Lord of Rest, Prayer and Mercy

Luke 6:1–19 · Sunday 15 November 2026 · St Paul’s Anglican Church Kogarah · For adult home groups · Sermon by Rev David Yung

Big Idea: Jesus is the Lord of Rest, the Lord of Prayer and the Lord of Mercy: true rest is not “I stop working” but “Christ has already finished the work for me”; prayer comes before action; and God often uses people who seem “impossible”.

Aim: by the end of the study, group members will

see that Jesus is Lord of the Sabbath, and that true rest is not “doing no work” but trusting the One who has already finished the work for us;

learn from Jesus’ night of prayer to bring their major decisions to the Father before they act;

take heart that Jesus chooses “unqualified” people, and bring their own withered places to him: “Stretch out your hand.”

You’ll need: Bibles (CSB), pens, about 60–75 minutes.

1. Connect (10 min)

  • Work emails at 10pm, the family group chat on a Sunday afternoon, the jobs list that never ends: when did you last feel truly rested? What stops your mind resting even when your body does?
  • Think of a big decision you have made (a job, a house, a school for the kids, caring for a parent). How did you actually decide: a pros-and-cons list, advice, gut feel, prayer?

2. Read: Luke 6:1–19 (CSB)

1 On a Sabbath, he passed through the grainfields. His disciples were picking heads of grain, rubbing them in their hands, and eating them.

2 But some of the Pharisees said, “Why are you doing what is not lawful on the Sabbath?”

3 Jesus answered them, “Haven’t you read what David did when he and those who were with him were hungry—

4 how he entered the house of God, and took and ate the bread of the Presence, which is not lawful for any but the priests to eat? He even gave some to those who were with him.”

5 Then he told them, “The Son of Man is Lord of the Sabbath.”

6 On another Sabbath he entered the synagogue and was teaching. A man was there whose right hand was shriveled.

7 The scribes and Pharisees were watching him closely, to see if he would heal on the Sabbath, so that they could find a charge against him.

8 But he knew their thoughts and told the man with the shriveled hand, “Get up and stand here.” So he got up and stood there.

9 Then Jesus said to them, “I ask you: Is it lawful to do good on the Sabbath or to do evil, to save life or to destroy it?”

10 After looking around at them all, he told him, “Stretch out your hand.” He did, and his hand was restored.

11 They, however, were filled with rage and started discussing with one another what they might do to Jesus.

12 During those days he went out to the mountain to pray and spent all night in prayer to God.

13 When daylight came, he summoned his disciples, and he chose twelve of them, whom he also named apostles:

14 Simon, whom he also named Peter, and Andrew his brother; and James, John, Philip, and Bartholomew;

15 Matthew and Thomas; James the son of Alphaeus, and Simon called the Zealot;

16 Judas the son of James, and Judas Iscariot, who became a traitor.

17 After coming down with them, he stood on a level place with a large crowd of his disciples and a great number of people from all Judea and Jerusalem and from the seacoast of Tyre and Sidon.

18 They came to hear him and to be healed of their diseases; and those tormented by unclean spirits were made well.

19 The whole crowd was trying to touch him, because power was coming out from him and healing them all.

3. Context

Luke 6:1–19 follows a run of clashes between Jesus and the Pharisees (5:17–39). God gave the Sabbath as a gift of rest (Genesis 2:3; Exodus 20:8–11), but the oral law (later written down in the Mishnah) listed 39 kinds of work forbidden on the Sabbath, including “reaping” and “threshing”. The Law of Moses itself allowed a hungry person to pluck heads of grain by hand (Deuteronomy 23:25). After two Sabbath disputes, Jesus prays all night, chooses the Twelve, and comes down to a level place where crowds from as far as Tyre and Sidon press in to touch him.

4. Discuss

  1. (vv.1–2) What were the disciples doing, and what was the Pharisees’ charge? The Law of Moses never forbade it (Deuteronomy 23:25), so where did their rule come from? Where are we tempted to turn our own traditions into rules for judging others?
  2. (vv.3–5) Jesus answers in two steps: David, then v.5. What is he claiming when he says, “The Son of Man is Lord of the Sabbath”? Why is that an astonishing claim?
  3. (vv.1–5, application) The sermon told of a busy mum who learned to pray while cooking and sing while settling the children: rest as “having Christ in the work”. What would that look like in your week at work, at home or caring for family?
  4. (vv.6–9) Why were the scribes and Pharisees watching Jesus so closely? Why does Jesus make the man stand in the middle, and what does their silence after his question in v.9 reveal about their hearts?
  5. (vv.10–11) How is the man healed? What does it mean that true rest is not “doing no work” but “letting God do the work”? Where in your life do you feel “withered”, unable to fix yourself?
  6. (vv.11–12) The Pharisees are filled with rage. What does Jesus do, and not do, in response? Luke often shows Jesus praying before big moments (5:16; 6:12; 9:18; 22:41). How do you usually make big decisions, and what would change if prayer came first?
  7. (vv.13–16) Look at the list of the Twelve. Who are they, and why is a tax collector (Matthew) and a Zealot (Simon) in the same team so striking? What does that say to a church whose members hold different political views?
  8. (vv.17–19) Jesus comes down to “a level place”, and power comes out from him to heal all who come. What does this downward movement tell us about the gospel? What would it mean for you to come near him this week, not because you deserve it but because he is willing?

5. Live It Out

KOG 3·2·1·0 — pray for 3, connect with 2, invite 1, 0 left unloved.

  • Pray for 3 — three people at work, in your street or in your extended family who don’t yet know Jesus. Pray for them by name every day.
  • Connect with 2 — a coffee, a meal or a walk with two of them this week. Listen for where they are weary.
  • Invite 1 — invite one of your three to our Community Christmas Concert on 13 December (we are praying for 120+), to the Welcome Home — Discover course, or to Christianity Explored (in Mandarin).
  • 0 left unloved — at morning tea, look for the person on the edge and welcome them the way Jesus came down to the level place.

This week’s challenge: Before one decision this week, big or small, set aside unhurried time, switch off your phone and ask, “Lord, which way do you want me to go?” And set one boundary around the Lord’s Day: not because the law requires it, but because you trust the Lord of the Sabbath holds tomorrow.

Memory verse: “The Son of Man is Lord of the Sabbath.” (Luke 6:5)

6. Pray

  • Thank Jesus, the Lord of the Sabbath, that rest is a gift of grace we receive in him. Confess where we have turned God’s gifts into rules, judged others by them, or relied on our own work.
  • Ask the Father for wisdom in the decisions in front of us, and for hearts that listen more than they speak. Pray for three new mid-week Growth Groups, and for the Community Christmas Concert on 13 December.
  • Pray by name for your 3: that they would come near Jesus and find rest in him, and for an opportunity to invite one of them.

7. Leader’s Notes

Framing. Joyfully point to Jesus. Guests and not-yet-Christians may be present; never corner anyone. The good news is not “try harder to rest” but that the Lord of the Sabbath has finished the work for us. Let people name their weariness and their “withered” places without shame.

Question helps

Q1 — The disciples picked and rubbed heads of grain. The oral law counted picking as “reaping” and rubbing as “threshing”, two of its 39 forbidden categories of work. But Moses never forbade it; Deuteronomy 23:25 allows it. The Pharisees replaced God’s law with human tradition, then used that tradition to condemn others. Examples: church customs, “how we always do it”, spiritual habits we use to measure others.

Q2 — David, God’s chosen king, ate the bread of the Presence because of real need, and God did not condemn him: “If David could, so can my disciples, because I am greater than David.” Then v.5: only God, who instituted the Sabbath (Genesis 2:3; Exodus 20:8–11), can define it. Jesus is in effect saying, “I am the God who instituted the Sabbath,” and “I am the one the Sabbath points to.”

Q3 — True rest is “finding rest in God”, not “doing nothing”. It arrives not through keeping rules but through trusting and following Jesus. Help people find concrete moments: the commute, the kitchen, the hospital waiting room.

Q4 — They wanted a charge against him (v.7). Jesus knew their thoughts, took the initiative and put the man in front of everyone. Their silence exposed that they cared about keeping the rules, not doing good and showing mercy. If the Sabbath is for people to be set free, healed and rested, refusing to heal is what truly breaks it.

Q5 — No medicine, no massage, no surgery: one word, and the hand was restored. The man could not heal himself, but when he obeyed and stretched out his hand, God’s power came. So rest is not “I stop working” but “I let Christ work in me”. Mark 3:5 says Jesus’ look held anger and grief at their hard hearts.

Q6 — No counter-attack, no rallying the crowd, no strategy: he spent all night in prayer. Choosing the Twelve was the starting point of the church’s history. His prayer was real dependence (Hebrews 5:7). Tim Keller: “If you don’t pray, you have no power. If you don’t pray for long periods, you have no deep power.” The biggest decisions are made in prayer, not by big data.

Q7 — Fishermen with no rabbinic training, a tax collector seen as a traitor, a Zealot from a violent resistance movement, and Judas who betrayed him. God makes instruments out of the willing, “so that no one may boast”. If Jesus could take in both Matthew and Simon, can I take in my brother? A church that cannot live out unity across political divides loses her witness.

Q8 — The gospel always moves downward: heaven to earth, throne to manger, glory to cross. Luke’s “level place” foreshadows Christ standing where we stand to save us. Power flowed from him “like water from a spring, like blood from a wound”: grace, not performance. The crowd were not worthy; God had mercy on their need.
