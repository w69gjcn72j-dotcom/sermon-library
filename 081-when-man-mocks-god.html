Intro:

I remember a few years ago, I took my children to a science museum in Canberra.

During one of the seminars, the presenter used the phrase, “Flying Spaghetti Monster” to describe something fake.

Of course, there’s no such thing called the Flying Spaghetti Monster.

All the children in the show would laugh at the idea.

However, there is a small number of vocal activists who use this phrase to describe God.

How can you believe in God, they said, it’s as silly as believing in a Flying Spaghetti Monster!

It’s an insult to God; not the Christian God, but to every religion as well.

When I heard the phrase used in a science museum, I was offended, and I wrote a letter of complaint to the Science Museum.

They apologised and promised to not use that phrase anymore.

It’s been many years since then; I trust that they’ve kept their word.

However, we should not be surprised if we see people mocking God or insulting God again in our society.

That’s the kind of society we live in.

Even though the majority of our society believe in God, or at least respectful of those who do believe in God,

there are a few who aren’t respectful; a few who would openly mock God.

They would put themselves as the judge of all things and judge even God himself.

That’s what I want us to think about today.

Do I judge God? Or do I worship God?

In today’s passage, we meet a group, no, a crowd, who would judge and mock God rather than worshipping God.

Please open up your Bible with me to Mark 15, beginning from verse 1.

In today’s talk, we’ll see three things:

Man judges God

God substitutes for Man

Follow the God-Man-King

Man judges God

Last week, we read that Jesus was betrayed and put on trial before the Sandhedrin,

the religious leaders of Jesus’ day.

They charged him with blasphemy, that is speaking falsely of God, and condemned him to death.

However, they didn’t have the power to execute him.

And so they took him to Pontius Pilate.

This is the Pontius Pilate that we often refer to in the Apostle’s Creed.

In the Apostle’s Creed, we say Jesus suffered under Pontius Pilate.

Pilate was the governor of Jerusalem in Jesus’ days.

According to records outside of the Bible, Pilate was known as a harsh governor, often treating the Jews ruthlessly to get things done.

He had a lot of political power in Jerusalem, and he was charged to keep the Jews under control.

When there was a dispute, they would bring them before Pilate.

Pilate and Roman governors like him would begin the legal proceedings from dawn.

That’s where we find ourselves at the beginning of chapter 15; look with me at Mark 15:1, “As soon as it was morning, having held a meeting with the elders, scribes, and the whole Sanhedrin, the chief priests tied Jesus up, led him away, and handed him over to Pilate.”

Pilate would’ve been on a seat known as the bema seat.

It’s a seat where judges sit to deliver their verdict on a case.

This reminds me a little of a TV show I used to watch.

It’s Justice Bao. Has anyone seen it?

Justice Bao would be presented with all types of cases and he would investigate carefully and justly and deliver perfect justice every time.

It’s awesome! I mean, look at the guy, he doesn’t mess around!

Sadly, the same couldn’t be said of Pontius Pilate.

The trial against Jesus was a setup from the start.

On the night before, the Sanhedrin had brought in all these false charges against Jesus.

And as soon as it’s morning, the Sanhedrin gathered to hand Jesus over to Pilate.

Judas handed Jesus over to the Sanhedrin and now the Sanhedrin hands Jesus over to Pilate.

On the night before, the Sanhedrin charged Jesus with blasphemy, of speaking falsely against God.

While blasphemy is bad, it’s not enough to condemn Jesus to death.

And so, the Sanhedrin adjusted the charge to make it more political.

Rather than blasphemy, they charge Jesus with being a traitor.

Being a nation under the rule of the Romans, the Jews only have one king, namely the Emperor Tiberius.

To claim to be a king in Jerusalem would be high treason.

And so, Pilate, sitting on his bema seat, asks Jesus, “Are you the King of the Jews?”

Is Jesus leading a revolution against the Romans?

Listen to how Jesus reply, “You say so”.

Jesus here is simply affirming what Pontius Pilate had said.

He simply acknowledges that he’s been accused of high treason.

It’s not an outright affirmation or else Pilate could’ve executed him straight away.

Jesus is saying, “I’m the King of the Jews? That’s what you’ve said.”

The chief priests then accuse Jesus of many things and Pilate asks Jesus again, “Aren’t you going to answer? Look how many things they are accusing you?”

But Jesus stays silent.

Though the chief priests try to convince that Jesus is worthy of death, Pilate doesn’t believe it.

He knows that it’s out of envy that the chief priests had handed Jesus over to him.

Pilate himself is not convinced that Jesus should be condemned to death.

He lobbies for Jesus at least three times; three times he tries to free Jesus.

He even offers to release Jesus to them, as part of his custom during the Passover.

But the crowd would have none of it.

They want Jesus to die by crucifixion.

How would Pilate respond?

Look with me at verse 15, “Wanting to satisfy the crowd, Pilate released Barabbas to them; and after having Jesus flogged, he handed him over to be crucified.”

Pilate’s verdict to Jesus are these frightening words, “Ibis in crucem (you shall mount the cross)”

Crucifixion is a horrible way to die.

The writer of Mark does not dwell on it because many of his first readers would’ve heard about it or seen it for themselves.

The Romans would nail the person to be crucified on a plank of wood.

And they would then just let them die a slow, horrible death.

Sometimes, it takes the victim a couple of days to die.

The victims would be placed in very public places so everyone can see what would happen if anyone dared to defy Rome.

Crucifixion’s so horrible that the famous Roman statesman Cicero said, “the very word cross should be far removed from not only from the person of a Roman citizen but from his thoughts, his eyes and his ears”.

Roman citizens were not practiced on Roman citizens; it’s far too horrible for a Roman to suffer death by crucifixion.

Only slave and robbers were crucified.

And here, Jesus, is sentenced to crucifixion.

Before Jesus goes on the cross, he had to face what’s known as the flagellum, the flogging.

It involves using a leather whip with pieces of metal or bone attached.

And the soldiers flogging can beat as many times as he wanted.

The pain from the whipping was so bad that some people actually die from this before they make it to the cross.

That’s what Jesus endured before he went to the cross.

As well as being whipped, Jesus was struck on the head, spat upon and mocked as the King of Jews.

They mocked him by putting a purple robe on him and crowning him with a crown of twisted thorns.

Even when they finally nailed him to the cross, they kept mocking him.

Look with me at the end of the passage, beginning from verse 29, ‘“Ha! The one who would destroy the temple and rebuild it in three days, 30 save yourself by coming down from the cross! 31 In the same way, the chief priests with the scribes were mocking him among themselves and saying, “He saved others, but he cannot save himself! 32 Let the Messiah, the King of Israel, come down now from the cross, so that we may see and believe.” Even those who were crucified with him taunted him.’

Even while Jesus was on the cross, they won’t stop taunting him.

They mock Jesus even as he’s on the cross.

They put themselves in the bema judgement seat as well.

They get to decide Jesus’ fate; they get to decide who Jesus is.

They’re Jesus’ judge; they’re the judge of the one who claim to be the Son of God.

Even today, there are many people who want to be Jesus’ judge.

Like Pontius Pilate before them, they want to seat in judgement of Jesus.

I still can’t believe that there are still people today who don’t think that Jesus exists.

There’s general consensus among most scholars that Jesus did exist.

I’ve travelled to the places that we just read about in the Bible.

Here’s the church of the Sepulchre, where Jesus is said to have been crucified and buried.

Excavations under the Church between 1961 and 1980 strongly suggest that this was indeed the site of Golgotha.

If you have three minutes, then can I suggest that you check out the site: Jesus: History’s Biggest Hoax (question mark)?

This is a very short video on why there are good reasons to believe that Jesus exist and why there is reason to believe that he doesn’t exist.

Billions of people from all around the world have believed in Jesus over the past two thousand years.

Yet there are still a number of people who want to be the judge of Jesus.

There are still many people want to be God’s judge.

They want to seat in the bema judgement seat and judge God.

Sometimes, even Christians can fall into the same trap, can’t we?

We judge which part of the Bible we listen to and which part we ignore.

We judge what commands of God we want to obey, and which we won’t.

We, in our natural, sinful state, want to judge God.

Friends, be careful about falling into the temptation of judging God and judging Jesus.

We are not in a position to judge God.

In fact, we’ve never been in a position to judge God.

The first humans, Adam and Eve, decided to judge God by rejecting his command.

They were kicked out of God’s Garden.

To judge God is to put yourself in the path of God’s judgement.

It’s a dangerous, fatal place to be.

But that’s the place where Jesus, God’s Son, has resolved to go.

Jesus is going to the place of judgement not because he deserved it, but because he’s chosen to be our substitute for God’s judgement.

God substitutes for men

Jesus goes to the cross to take be our substitute; Jesus has gone to the cross to take the place of sinners.

Usually, we substitute like for like.

For example, when Marcus Williams was sick on school picture day, his identical twin brother, Malcolm Williams, took a picture for him instead.

Being identical, the teachers didn’t notice the difference!

That’s a substitute of like for like.

But when Jesus substitutes for us, it’s a completely different swap.

We are sinners but Jesus has no sin; we deserve God’s judgment and Jesus doesn’t; Jesus belong to heaven but we don’t.

And yet, out of his love for us, God sends his own Son to be the substitute for us.

We see this illustrated even as Pontius Pilate was trying to free Jesus.

During the Passover, it was Pilate’s custom to set a prisoner free.

On the morning that Jesus was put on trial, there was already another prisoner waiting for crucifixion.

His name was Barabbas, which means son of the father, or son of the teacher.

And so, Pilate has two men with him, a Barabbas, son of the father, and Jesus, son of the God the Father.

One’s already been shown to be a sinner, a murderer but the other is clearly shown to be innocent.

Who goes free?

The murderer Barabbas goes free while the innocent Jesus is sentence to crucifixion.

At that moment, God demonstrates the significance of Jesus’ death for us.

In Jesus, death, Christ died for sinners.

That’s what we read in Romans 5:8, “But God proves his own love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us.”

If you want to see proof of God’s love, take a look at Jesus on the cross.

On the cross, Jesus, the Holy One, died for the unholy ones, sinners like us.

The Apostle Peter summarises it clearly in 1 Peter 2:24, “He himself bore our sins in his body on the tree; so that, having died to sins, we might live for righteousness. By his wounds, you have been healed.”

Jesus himself bore our sins on the tree, on the cross.

When Jesus was whipped, struck, nailed, wounded, he was wounded for us so that by his wounds we might be healed.

And again, in 1 Peter 3:18, “For Christ also suffered for sins once for all, the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring you to God.”

Jesus, the righteous one had die for the unrighteous ones.

Because Jesus substituted for me, I have life instead of death, reconciliation instead of rejection, love from God instead of hate from God.

In the Christian classic, the Pilgrim’s Progress, the author John Bunyan writes the story about a man called Christian.

At the beginning of the story, Christian carries a huge burden on his back.

Christian was reading a book, and as read, he wept and trembled.

He read of his sin and didn’t know what to do with it.

He then starts on a journey to get rid of his sack.

He then came upon this place with a cross.

Here’s what happens when he approach the cross, “Just as Christian came up with the cross, his burden loosed from off his shoulders, and fell from off his back; and began to tumble, and so continued to do so until it came to the mouth of the sepulchre, where it fell in, and I saw it no more.”

After his burden comes off, he sings, “Thus far did I come laden with my sin, nor could aught ease the grief that I was in, till I came hither. What a place is this! …Blessed cross! Blessed sepulchre! Blessed rather be the Man that there was put to shame for me.”

That’s what happens when a Christian; Jesus was put to shame for us!

Jesus became our substitute for us!

Though we have judged God and deserved to be judged by God, God saved us by making Jesus the substitute for our sins.

It’s by his wound that we’re healed.

Follow the God-Man-King

What does this mean for us?

We need to follow the God, who is Man, who is also King.

Jesus has said in Mark 8:34, “If anyone wants to follow after me, let him deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me.”

Jesus is not talking about doing your daily chores and going through the hardships of life.

He’s talking about following him, wherever he goes, even if it means death by crucifixion.

As soon as Jesus was flogged, he was given the plank of wood to carry to Golgotha, the place where they would crucify him.

Jesus was probably physically unable to carry the cross after the flogging and so Simon of Cyrene carried the cross for Jesus to Golgotha.

When I was in Year seven, I had a friend called Martin.

Martin had a bad fall and had hurt his ankle badly.

As a result, he had to walk around in crutches.

To help him, I carried his very big bag around school for him.

I didn’t know what he had put in there but it was sure heavier than my bag!

The piece of wood that Simon carried around would’ve been a lot heavier.

Now we don’t know whether Simon was a Christian or whether he would one day become a Christian,

But what he was doing was what every Christian is called to do.

We’re to carry our cross and follow Jesus.

After Jesus was mocked as the King of the Jews, he was led straight to crucifixion.

It’s exactly what Jesus had said would happen, time and time again.

After Peter had confessed that Jesus was the Christ, Jesus prophesied that he would be killed.

What Jesus had said was fulfilled.

But as well as death, Jesus has prophesied life beyond death.

Just as Jesus had said he would die, Jesus had said he would rise again.

Even as Jesus was going to the cross, bruised and battered, slowly walking to his death, there was hope.

Death is not the end; resurrection will come.

This is the hope that all Christians have.

Yes, there will be suffering that comes with the cross but beyond the suffering there is resurrection.

There is hope.

For many of us, last year was a tiring year.

This time last year, we weren’t allowed to meet physically.

Many of us have spent many hours, many sleepless nights, to keep the Church going.

It was hard work but I’m very thankful to God for those who have persevered.

That’s part of carrying the cross of Christ.

It’s hard work but it’s life-saving work, it’s hope-inspiring work.

As we continue as a Church, we need to keep asking ourselves, what does it mean for me to follow Christ at this moment in my life?

What does carrying my cross look like today?

When we do carry the cross, we shouldn’t carry it like Simon the Cyrene.

We don’t carry the cross because we’re forced to.

Rather we carry it willingly because we’re thankful for what Jesus has done for us.

What does carrying the cross look like today?

Let’s spend a moment thinking about it.

Please talk with the person next to you.

You can message me via Wechat, whatsapp or Zoom.

We have twenty seconds.

What does carrying the cross look like today?

It means to stop judging God and to start following God.

Let God be God.

On Friday, there was a car accident outside the Church.

An old gentlemen was hurt in the car.

Some of the passers by were helping the gentleman while I call for the ambulance.

They came soon after and we let them look after the gentleman instead.

They were far more qualified to look after the gentleman than us.

They were trained for it; they were the ambos.

God has come in the person of Jesus. Let God be God.

Let him be who he is; let him be God.

Stop judging God.

Stop judging on who God is and what he is allowed to have on my life.

Stop judging God and restricting him on asking the kind of good things that he wants to see in our lives.

Let God be God; listen to him, respect him as God.

Ask God for the strength to follow him.

Ask God to help us repent and ask him to show us how to serve him.

Stop judging God and start following God.

Here are the three questions for this week:

How am I judging God rather than worshipping God?

What does carrying the cross look like in my life right now?

In what area of my life must I let God be God?

Friends, Jesus, the Son of God, has become a substitute for us to save us.

Let’s take up our cross and follow him.

Let’s pray
