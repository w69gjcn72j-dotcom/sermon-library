The Foolish Wisdom of the Cross of Christ

1 Corinthians 1:18–25 · 16 August 2026

Big Idea: Don't be a wise-sounding fool but a foolish-sounding sage, for God is pleased to save all who believe his message of Christ crucified.

Good morning, brothers and sisters. It is good to be with you.

Let me begin with a question. Has anyone ever made you feel a little foolish for believing in Jesus?

Perhaps a grandchild said, kindly enough, “Nobody really believes that anymore, Grandma.” Perhaps someone smiled politely when you said you would pray for them, and you could see they thought you sweet, but simple. Perhaps it was someone you loved for fifty years who never came to trust the Lord.

None of us likes to look foolish. And our message does sound foolish: a man nailed to a Roman cross two thousand years ago, and his death the only hope any of us has.

This is nothing new. The Christians in Corinth felt the same pressure, so Paul wrote to them — and this morning his words are for us. Three things.

1. Man's wise-sounding problem with the Cross of Christ.

2. God's foolish-sounding answer in the Cross of Christ.

3. Be foolish-sounding sages who preach and praise Christ crucified.

Let's pray. “Speak, O Lord, and by your Holy Spirit help us to listen, that we may trust you and live a life that brings you glory. For Christ's sake, Amen.”

1. Man's wise-sounding problem with the Cross of Christ

Verse 18:

“For the word of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but it is the power of God to us who are being saved.”

One message. Two opposite responses.

To those who are perishing, the cross is an embarrassment. Notice Paul does not say they have perished. They are perishing. It is not finished yet — which is why we still pray, and still speak.

But hear the other half of that verse as your own: “the power of God to us who are being saved.” Being saved. Present tense.

Some of you trusted the Lord Jesus sixty, seventy, eighty years ago, and it can feel as though the important part of your Christian life is behind you. It is not. At the cross, Jesus saved you. He is saving you still — this morning, in this chair, in the long night when sleep will not come. And he will come to save you finally, and take you home. God has not finished his work in you.

Then Paul quotes Isaiah in verse 19: “I will destroy the wisdom of the wise.” God is not against learning. He is against the wisdom that says, “I know better than God.” Verse 20 asks: where is the scholar who can argue God down? Nowhere. To God it is empty talk.

God's answer is in verse 21:

“For since, in God's wisdom, the world did not know God through wisdom, God was pleased to save those who believe through the foolishness of what is preached.”

God was pleased. He was delighted to do it this way. Not clever arguments, not displays of power — preaching. One person simply telling another about Jesus.

Think how that message reached you. Very likely not through a famous man, but a mother who prayed at your bedside, a Sunday School teacher whose name you barely remember, a hymn you learned at seven and can still sing. Weak, ordinary means — and here you are, still trusting him. That is the power of God.

2. God's foolish-sounding answer in the Cross of Christ

Verses 22 and 23:

“For the Jews ask for signs and the Greeks seek wisdom, but we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to the Jews and foolishness to the Gentiles.”

The word for stumbling block is skandalon — where we get our word scandal. To the Jew the cross was a scandal: God's own chosen King, executed like a criminal? They wanted a sign of power. To the Greek it was simply foolish, so they laughed.

We are not so different. Two things people still say.

The first: “If God does this for me, then I will believe.” That is asking for a sign. Some of you have prayed for healing, and it has not come. Some have prayed for years for a child still far from the Lord. I will not pretend that is easy. But hear this carefully. God has already shown you his power, at the cross. He did not spare his own Son. If he gave you that, he has held nothing back. Your unanswered prayer is not the measure of his love. Calvary is.

The second: “I don't understand it, so it can't be true.” That is the Greek. But a God small enough for me to fully explain would not be worth trusting.

So Paul refuses to give people what they demand. He gives them what they need: Christ crucified, plainly. And so must we, however old-fashioned it sounds.

3. Be foolish-sounding sages who preach and praise Christ crucified

Verse 24:

“Yet to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ is the power of God and the wisdom of God.”

We do not get a different message from the one the world laughs at. We get the same message — and come to the opposite conclusion. Where they see weakness, we see the power of God; where they see foolishness, the wisdom of God.

Picture two people at a window watching the rain. One grumbles that the day is spoiled; the other is a farmer at the end of a long drought, thanking God with tears on his face. The rain has not changed. What differs is what each one treasures.

So it is with the cross. What we make of it shows what our hearts treasure — and hearts are only changed when God calls us. To be called means to be chosen by God for salvation. So if you love the cross this morning, do not congratulate yourself. Thank him. You are not better; you are called.

Paul calls such people sages — the old word for the wise ones. Fools in the world's eyes; wise in God's. Verse 25 tells us why:

“Because God's foolishness is wiser than human wisdom, and God's weakness is stronger than human strength.”

When we are at our strongest, God is stronger still. When we are at our wisest, God is wiser still.

Let me tell you about a sister in Christ, Elizabeth Foord, a dear friend of my wife Jenny, who went to be with the Lord recently. Elizabeth was tiny — shorter than Jenny, the shortest in our family. But she was mighty. At one stage she taught eight Scripture classes a week and visited people in twelve nursing homes. She was one of the wisest, strongest women we have ever met — and she is wiser and stronger now, because she is with the Lord Jesus.

Now some of you may be thinking: that was Elizabeth. I cannot teach classes. I cannot visit anybody. I can barely get down the corridor.

Then listen again to what the passage says. God saves through what looks weak. The power was never in the messenger; it was always in the message. A frail messenger does not weaken it one bit.

A word of thanks to the nurse who turns you in the night, and a reason given for your hope. A grandchild's name carried to God in prayer every day for thirty years. A hymn sung with a cracked voice. The way a Christian bears pain, and the way a Christian dies — with hope, without fear. That is preaching Christ crucified. That is not the retirement of your ministry; it is your ministry, bearing fruit you will not see until glory.

And please know this: your church has not forgotten you. We pray for you, we thank God for you, and we long to see you. You are not the church's past. You are the church's saints.

Conclusion

So why are we sometimes embarrassed by the message of the cross? Because it sounds weak and foolish to those around us. But to those who are perishing it is foolishness; to us who are called it is the power of God and the wisdom of God.

So, brothers and sisters, don't be a wise-sounding fool but a foolish-sounding sage, for God is pleased to save all who believe his message of Christ crucified.

Don't give up. Bear gladly whatever the world thinks of a crucified Saviour, and look to the day when God himself welcomes you home.

Let us pray

“Father, thank you for your mercy on people like us. We were perishing, and you called us and saved us. Forgive us when we think like the world and are ashamed of you. Turn our eyes again to Christ crucified — foolishness to some, but to us who are called, the power of God and the wisdom of God. Be near to each one here. Strengthen the weak, comfort the suffering, and keep us trusting you to the very end. In Jesus' name we pray. Amen.”

Three questions to think about

1. In what ways am I still tempted to be embarrassed by the message of Christ crucified?

2. What evidence am I asking God for — a sign of power, a persuasive argument, or am I content with the cross?

3. Who is on my heart today who is still perishing, and will I keep praying for God to call them?

Don't be a wise-sounding fool but a foolish-sounding sage, for God is pleased to save all who believe his message of Christ crucified.
