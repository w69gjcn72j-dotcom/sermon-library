The Foolish Wisdom of the Cross of Christ

1 Corinthians 1:18–25 · Sunday 16 August 2026 · St Paul’s Anglican Kogarah

Big Idea: Don’t be a wise-sounding fool but a foolish-sounding sage, for God is pleased to save all who believe his message of Christ crucified.

Aim: By the end of this study, students will (1) see that the message of the cross splits the world into two groups — those perishing and those being saved; (2) understand that God deliberately chose a message and a method that look foolish, so that nobody boasts except in Christ crucified; (3) take one concrete step this week to say something plain about Jesus and to pray for 3 friends who don’t know him yet.

You’ll need: Bibles (CSB), pens, this sheet, and about 60–75 minutes.

Connect (10 min)

Pick one (or both) to get talking:

  • The only one cheering. Tell about a time you were the only one backing something — a team, a band, an opinion — while everyone else went quiet. What made you keep going, or made you stop?
  • Too embarrassing to say. What is something you believe that is hard to say out loud at school, because of how people would react? Why does embarrassment have so much power over us?

Today’s passage is about a message that has always been embarrassing — and about why God chose it on purpose.

Read — 1 Corinthians 1:18–25 (CSB)

18 For the word of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but it is the power of God to us who are being saved. 19 For it is written, I will destroy the wisdom of the wise, and I will set aside the intelligence of the intelligent. 20 Where is the one who is wise? Where is the teacher of the law? Where is the debater of this age? Hasn’t God made the world’s wisdom foolish? 21 For since, in God’s wisdom, the world did not know God through wisdom, God was pleased to save those who believe through the foolishness of what is preached.

22 For the Jews ask for signs and the Greeks seek wisdom, 23 but we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to the Jews and foolishness to the Gentiles. 24 Yet to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ is the power of God and the wisdom of God, 25 because God’s foolishness is wiser than human wisdom, and God’s weakness is stronger than human strength.

Context

Corinth was a city obsessed with status, and the church there had split into fan clubs behind their favourite speakers. In that world a good speaker was polished, clever and persuasive — and crucifixion was the most degrading death imaginable, reserved for slaves and rebels. To preach a crucified Messiah was to lose the argument before you opened your mouth.

Paul does not apologise for it. He says the message that looks like a disaster is exactly the place where God’s power is found — and that God set it up that way so that nobody could boast about being clever enough to work him out.

Discuss

  1. Read v.18. Paul says there are only two kinds of people in the world. What are they — and what is it that separates them?
  1. “Perishing” and “being saved” are both present tense: they are happening right now. What difference does that make to how you think about the people you sit next to in class?
  1. Verse 19 quotes Isaiah 29:14. Read Isaiah 29:13 as well. What kind of wisdom is God destroying — and what kind is he not?
  1. Verse 20 asks three questions and expects the answer “nowhere”. Who are the “wise”, the “teachers of the law” and the “debaters” in your world — the voices that sound unanswerable?
  1. Verse 21 says God “was pleased” to save people through “the foolishness of what is preached”. Why would God choose a method that looks weak rather than one that looks impressive?
  1. Verses 22–23: the Jews wanted signs, the Greeks wanted arguments. Which are you more like — “show me something” or “explain it until it makes sense”? What would it take to trust God without either?
  1. Verse 24 says the very same message is power and wisdom “to those who are called”. If the message does not change, what has to change for someone to hear it differently?
  1. Verse 25: “God’s foolishness is wiser than human wisdom.” Where at school this week will you have to choose between looking clever and being counted with Christ crucified?

Live It Out

Being a Christian at school will sometimes make you look foolish. That is not a sign something has gone wrong — Paul says to expect it. Here is what it looks like this week:

  • Say one plain thing. Don’t wait for the perfect conversation or the killer argument. Say one true, plain sentence about Jesus to one person — that is preaching, and God is pleased to use it.
  • Stop editing the edges off. Notice where you soften the gospel to make yourself easier to like. Sin, the cross, judgement, the resurrection. Don’t soften the scandal; say it kindly and plainly.
  • KOG 3·2·1·0. Pray for your 3 friends who don’t know Jesus yet, connect with 2 people at church or youth group this week, invite 1 friend to KOGYouth, church or the Father’s Day celebration (6 September) — and leave 0 people unloved or left out at school.

This week’s challenge: When someone says something dismissive about Christianity this week, don’t argue them into a corner and don’t go silent. Say one honest sentence about why the cross matters to you — then tell someone in your group that you did it.

Memory verse: “For the word of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but it is the power of God to us who are being saved.” — 1 Corinthians 1:18 (CSB)

Pray

  • Thank God for the power in the message. Thank him that the cross is not a weak option or a Plan B — it is the power of God to save everyone who believes.
  • Pray for your 3 by name. Go around the group and each pray for your three friends who aren’t Christians yet — that God’s kindness would lead them to repentance, and for a chance to point one of them to Jesus.
  • Pray for our church. Ask God for our three new mid-week Growth Groups, for six additional qualified helpers for KOGKids, KOGMini and KOGYouth, and for the Father’s Day celebration on 6 September (“A Father’s Invitation”) — that many would come and hear Christ crucified.

Leader’s Notes

Warm framing: some of your students may not yet be Christians, and this passage is a gift to them. It does not ask them to be clever enough or good enough — it says the message will sound foolish, and God saves people through it anyway. The tone is not “try harder to believe” but “look at what God was pleased to do”. Never corner anyone. Let the passage do the work.

Expect two wrong turns. Some students will hear “the world calls it foolish” and conclude that Christians should be deliberately weird or rude. Others will conclude that arguments and thinking do not matter at all. Verse 21 answers both: God is not against thinking, he is against a wisdom that will not bow to him — and the answer is not rudeness but plain, kind proclamation.

Question helps

  • Q1: Two groups — those perishing and those being saved. What separates them is not intelligence or background but what they make of the word of the cross.
  • Q2: Both are present tense. Perishing is not yet final; being saved covers past, present and future. So the classmate who laughs today is not beyond God’s reach, and your own salvation is something Jesus is still carrying you through.
  • Q3: God is not destroying scholarship or careful thought. Isaiah 29:13 names it: lips that honour God while the heart is far away, worship run by human rules. It is wisdom that will not submit to God.
  • Q4: Let them name real voices — teachers, influencers, group chats, comment sections. The point is not that these people are stupid, but that no one can finally out-argue God.
  • Q5: Because a method that looks impressive would produce people boasting in the method (and in themselves). God chose preaching — plain proclamation — so that the credit is unmistakably his.
  • Q6: Both demands make us the judge. Jesus refused to give the sign-seekers what they asked for, offering only the sign of Jonah — his death and resurrection. Faith is trusting the God who has already shown his power at the cross.
  • Q7: The person has to be called by God. That is God’s work, not our persuasiveness — which is exactly why we pray for our three friends as hard as we speak to them.
  • Q8: Push for specifics: a group chat, a class discussion, a friend who asks. Looking clever is not a sin; choosing it over Christ is.

Every one of those depends on the same thing this passage is about: God saving people through a message that sounds foolish. Growth groups are where that gets practised, not just discussed — make room for students to say what is hard, and pray for each other by name.
