Nursing Home Talks

How the Gospel Demolishes the Self and Rebuilds a Life

Galatians 2:15–21 · 23 August 2026

Big Idea: The gospel does not renovate the self; it puts the old self to death and gives us Christ himself as our life — no longer I, but Christ.

Good morning, brothers and sisters. It is good to be with you.

Most of you have built something in your lifetime. A home. A family. A good name in your street or your trade. Years of quiet service in a church.

But underneath all of that, every one of us has been building something else. Call it the house of the self. Some laid the foundation with what they achieved. Some built the walls out of good character — I never cheated anyone, I raised my children properly. Some put a roof of religious devotion on top. And we never stop patching and painting, to prove one thing: I am a person of worth.

And in our passage this morning Paul issues the most subversive demolition order in history. That house must come down. Not renovated. Not propped up. Pulled up by the roots. And in its place, not a better self — but Christ himself as our life.

Three things.

1. The only foundation — faith in Christ, not works of the law.

2. Demolition and rebuilding — the law leads to death, and death leads to life.

3. The central declaration — no longer I, but Christ.

Let's pray. “Speak, O Lord, and by your Spirit help us to hear, that we may rest in Christ alone. For his sake, Amen.”

1. The only foundation: faith in Christ (vv. 15–16)

Verse 16 says:

“…a person is not justified by the works of the law, but by faith in Jesus Christ.”

Justified is a courtroom word. It does not mean God makes me into a nice person. It means God declares the case closed, the charge dropped, my standing changed. Not guilty.

Why can the law not do this? Because it was given to reveal sin, not to remove it. The law is a mirror: it shows you the dirt on your face, but it cannot wash it off.

And notice Paul's logic. Even he — a Jew, with the law and the covenant and the whole religious inheritance — had to believe in Christ. No exceptions, no shortcuts, and no long-service benefits for old Christians.

I knew of a man who served his church for fifty years — Sunday School, a small group, and he helped build the hall with his own hands. Deep down he carried a quiet security: I have done so much, God must surely accept me. Then this verse shone its light on him. Even good things, made the foundation of your standing with God, become works of the law — and a heart built on that is never at rest. Not because you have not done enough, but because you built on sand.

So he learned to say each morning: I am not justified by my service. I am justified by faith in Christ. I served out of thankfulness.

Now hear why this matters so much here. Some of you can no longer serve at all. You cannot teach, or visit, or carry a plate to the hall. If your standing with God had ever depended on those things, you would be in real trouble this morning. But it never did — not once. It rests where it has always rested, on Christ, and he has not changed.

2. Demolition and rebuilding (vv. 17–19)

In verse 18 Paul says, “If I rebuild what I once tore down, I prove that I am a lawbreaker.” What had he torn down? The whole system of being made right by law-keeping. To build it up again is to say, “The death of Christ was not quite enough; I must add a little of my own.” That is an insult to the cross.

And then verse 19:

“For through the law I died to the law, so that I might live for God.”

The law plays the part of the executioner. It declares that I deserve to die; I accept the verdict; and so I die to the law. And once a person has died, the law has no more authority over them — as a widow is no longer bound by the marriage law. But death is not the goal. Living for God is the goal. So if you have died to the law, other people's expectations can no longer condemn you.

That is worth sitting with. Some of you carry a heavy fear of being a burden, and wonder what your children think when they see you like this. You remember who you used to be — capable, needed, in charge — and wonder what you are worth now.

Brothers and sisters, you have died to all of that. Their approval was never your righteousness, and its loss is not your condemnation. God has accepted you in Christ, and that is enough. Demolition: the law leads to death. Rebuilding: death leads to life.

3. No longer I, but Christ (vv. 20–21)

Now verse 20:

“I have been crucified with Christ and I no longer live, but Christ lives in me… who loved me and gave himself for me.”

“I have been crucified with Christ.” That is not me crucifying my own sin. It means we have been joined to him. When Christ died, God put me in him — my old self, my self-centredness, my attempts to justify myself, all nailed to the cross. And the verb is a perfect tense: it has already happened and stands permanently in force. Your old self is not slowly dying. It has died.

That does not mean sin has vanished. Paul himself spoke of two laws at war within him. So we still need the Spirit to put sin to death in us, day by day. Which is why a good prayer each morning is simply: “Lord, today I have no strength, but you are my strength. Live out in me a day that pleases you.”

“It is no longer I who live.” Paul does not mean he has been wiped out. He means the self-centred “I” no longer sits on the throne. The world says, discover yourself. The gospel says you only find your true self by losing it — for the self is the best-hidden idol of all.

“But Christ lives in me.” Not imitating Christ, not merely following his example — Christ himself living his life out through us. We cannot do that. He does it.

“The life I now live in the body.” We are not turned into spiritual ghosts. We still live ordinary lives in ordinary bodies — aching joints, failing eyes, days that feel very long. But the way we live has changed: no longer our own strength, but faith in the Son of God.

And then: “who loved me and gave himself for me.” Notice — for me. Not a love for humanity in the abstract. He died for particular people. For you.

I heard of a man who fell into deep self-loathing. His prayers were short, his memory poor, he forgot people's names, and he thought himself no use to anybody. He kept saying, “I am not worthy to be a Christian.”

Then God spoke to him through this verse. You have already been crucified with Christ. That question — am I worthy? — has already been put to death. The one living now is not the you who cannot do things well; it is Christ living in you. Not because you are worthy. Because he loves you and gave himself for you.

And verse 21: “…if righteousness could be gained through the law, Christ died for nothing.” If I could be put right by my own record, the cross was unnecessary. But the cross proves the opposite. So it is either all of Christ, or all of you. There is no half and half.

Conclusion

Every religion in the world is like a drowning man taking hold of his own hair and trying to haul himself out of the water. The gospel is a pair of nail-scarred hands reaching down from above and lifting you out. You do not need to hold on to your own hair. You need only let go, and take hold of him.

So whatever is left of the house of the self, let it come down. It is not you who is demolished — it is that self which was always trying to earn its own standing. Christ does not come to live in a house you decorated for him. He comes to be your life.

And let me say this plainly before I finish. You are not the church's past; you are the church's saints. Christ lives in you this morning, and a life he is living is never a wasted one. We pray for you, we thank God for you, and we long to see you.

Let us pray

“Lord, we confess that we have spent our lives building our own righteousness — out of our work, our service, our respectability. Today we lay it down. We receive your cross, and this alone is our righteousness. Our old self has been crucified with Christ, and the life we now live is Christ living in us. He loved us and gave himself for us. Be near to each one here; strengthen the weak, comfort the suffering, and hold us fast to the end. In Jesus' name, Amen.”

Three questions to think about

1. Is the security of my faith Christ alone, or Christ plus my own performance?

2. When I am weak, do I fall into accusing myself, or do I rest in grace?

3. Am I still carrying it all myself, or letting Christ live his life in me?

No longer I, but Christ who lives in me — who loved me and gave himself for me.
