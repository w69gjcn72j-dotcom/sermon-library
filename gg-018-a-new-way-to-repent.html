Home Groups (adults)

A New Way to Repent

Luke 5:27–39 · Sunday 8 November 2026 · St Paul’s Anglican Church Kogarah · Sermon by Rev David Yung

Big Idea: Fellow sick sinners, Jesus calls us to a new repentance centred on him, to leave everything, follow him, feast with him and fast in his absence.

Aim: by the end of the study, group members will

see what genuine repentance looks like: Levi stands up and leaves everything to follow the Doctor, and Jesus calls us to repentance that is personal, not transactional;

understand why Jesus’ followers feast while the Bridegroom is here and fast in his absence, and why Jesus cannot be patched onto an old life;

name where they are staying seated, stop pretending to be healthy, and plan one concrete way to open their home or church to a sinner who needs Jesus.

You’ll need: Bibles (CSB), pens, about 60–75 minutes.

1. Connect (10 min)

  • When did someone last say “I’m sorry” and you didn’t believe them (a colleague, a tradesman, a family member)? What made it ring hollow? What would have made it real?
  • We all have our own ways of renaming sins: “I’m just being honest”, “righteous anger”, “sharing a prayer point”. Which one do you recognise in yourself (or in the workplace)?

2. Read: Luke 5:27–39 (CSB)

27 After this, he went out and saw a tax collector named Levi sitting at the tax office, and he said to him, “Follow me.”

28 So, leaving everything behind, he got up and began to follow him.

29 Then Levi hosted a grand banquet for him at his house. A large crowd of tax collectors and others were guests with them.

30 But the Pharisees and their scribes were complaining to his disciples, “Why do you eat and drink with tax collectors and sinners?”

31 Jesus replied to them, “It is not those who are healthy who need a doctor, but those who are sick.

32 I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance.”

33 Then they said to him, “John’s disciples fast often and say prayers, and those of the Pharisees do the same, but yours eat and drink.”

34 Jesus said to them, “You can’t make the wedding guests fast while the groom is with them, can you?

35 But the time will come when the groom will be taken away from them—then they will fast in those days.”

36 He also told them a parable: “No one tears a patch from a new garment and puts it on an old garment. Otherwise, not only will he tear the new, but also the piece from the new garment will not match the old.

37 And no one puts new wine into old wineskins. Otherwise, the new wine will burst the skins, it will spill, and the skins will be ruined.

38 No, new wine is put into fresh wineskins.

39 And no one, after drinking old wine, wants new, for he says, ‘The old is good.’”

3. Context

Luke 5:27–39 follows Jesus’ healing of the paralysed man and his claim to forgive sins (5:17–26). Levi was a Jew who collected taxes for Rome, the oppressors of his people, and probably charged more than Rome required; he was seen as a traitor. The Pharisees and their scribes, usually the guests of honour at such feasts, grumble that Jesus eats with such people. They also fast twice a week, far more than the one fast God commanded, on the Day of Atonement. Jesus answers with a doctor, a wedding, a patch and wineskins.

4. Discuss

  1. (vv.27–28) Levi was a hated tax collector, still at his booth when Jesus said, “Follow me.” What does Levi do? What does his response teach us about what repentance looks like when Jesus calls?
  2. (vv.27–28) Repentance here isn’t forced out of Levi; it blossoms from his life. How is repentance that is personal, a response to a Person, different from saying “sorry” to get out of trouble? Where do we say sorry to God but stay seated?
  3. (vv.29–32) What is the Pharisees’ complaint, and what does Jesus’ reply about a doctor tell us about who is sick? Why can only those who admit they are sick come to him for healing? What does “pretending to be healthy” look like for adults?
  4. (vv.29–32, application) Levi left his tax booth but opened his home. Where are we tempted, like the Pharisees, to grumble that others receive more grace than they deserve? How could your home, and our church, become a banquet for sinners who know they need healing?
  5. (vv.33–34) Why did the Pharisees think prayer and fasting, not feasting, were the right marks of repentance? What does Jesus mean by asking what time it is, and by calling himself the Bridegroom? (Think of God’s promise in Ezekiel 36:26.)
  6. (v.35) When will the disciples fast, and what does that point to? As Christians today, why should we do both, feast and fast? Where are you tempted to feast and forget a broken world, or to despair and forget the joy of Christ? Who needs you to remind them to feast, and who to fast?
  7. (vv.36–38) What do the new patch on an old garment and the new wine in old wineskins say about adding Jesus to an unchanged life? Why does the new way of repentance burst the old?
  8. (vv.36–39, application) Where do you tempt yourself to keep Jesus to one hour on Sundays, or to decide how much of life is his? What would it mean this week to let him be Lord of all of it?

5. Live It Out

KOG 3·2·1·0 — pray for 3, connect with 2, invite 1, 0 left unloved.

  • Pray for 3 — three people at work, in your street or in your extended family who don’t yet know Jesus. Pray for them by name every day.
  • Connect with 2 — a coffee, a meal or a walk with two of them this week.
  • Invite 1 — invite one of your three to our Community Christmas Concert on 13 December (we are praying for 120+), to the Welcome Home — Discover course, or to Christianity Explored (in Mandarin).
  • 0 left unloved — at morning tea, look for the person on the edge, and welcome them the way Jesus would.

This week’s challenge: Stop pretending to be healthy. This week, tell one trusted person in your group one area where you have been keeping Jesus as a patch, not as Lord, and ask them to pray for you. Then make your home a place of welcome: invite someone to a meal.

Memory verse: “It is not those who are healthy who need a doctor, but those who are sick. I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance.” (Luke 5:31–32)

6. Pray

  • Thank the Father for sending Jesus, our great Doctor, to sick sinners like us. Confess where we have stayed seated, pretended to be healthy, or grumbled that others got more grace than they deserved.
  • Ask the Holy Spirit to change our hearts from stone to flesh, to help us rejoice in Jesus’ presence, and to make us yearn for his return. Pray for the Community Christmas Concert on 13 December, and for the households God is bringing us.
  • Pray by name for your 3, that they would hear Jesus’ “Follow me” and stand up, and for one to be invited.

7. Leader’s Notes

Framing. Joyfully point to Jesus. Guests and not-yet-Christians may be present; never corner anyone. The good news is not “say sorry harder” but that the Doctor calls the sick and welcomes them to his table. Let people be honest without shame.

Question helps

Q1 — Levi’s job made him a traitor and a cheat in his people’s eyes. He did not hesitate: he got up, left everything (including his income) and followed Jesus. We have no record of what he said; his action spoke. Repentance means recognising we have been wrong and turning our minds and hearts back to God, and here it shows in how a person responds when Jesus calls: stay seated or stand up.

Q2 — Genuine godly repentance depends on our relationship with Jesus. It is personal, not transactional. If we are truly sorry to God, we will follow Jesus; if not, we stay seated. Examples: a sorry we say only when caught, or only when forced.

Q3 — The Pharisees complained that Jesus’ disciples ate with “tax collectors and sinners”. Jesus: the healthy don’t need a doctor, the sick do. We are all sick, and our physical sicknesses are symptoms of our spiritual sickness; a million sorries can’t heal us. Only those who admit they are sick will come to him; the “righteous” won’t say sorry to Jesus and so are not forgiven. Jesus heals us so that we repent and turn back to God, not so that we can sin again.

Q4 — Levi opened his home and the church opens its doors. Invite the “tax collectors” and welcome them as Jesus would. The shunners were themselves shunned, and they grumbled about grace. Stop pretending to be healthy: repentance starts by admitting you are sick and hearing “Follow me”.

Q5 — The Pharisees fasted twice a week (104 days a year), while God commanded only one fast: the Day of Atonement, a national “Sorry Day”. Jesus asks what time it is: the Bridegroom is here. You don’t fast at a wedding. Sacrifices could not change hearts; God promised a new heart (Ezekiel 36:26) and Jesus, the Heart Surgeon, brings it.

Q6 — “When the groom is taken away” points to the cross. Now we feast, because God has forgiven and adopted us and Jesus is with us always; and we fast, because we long for his return. Repentance is both joy and yearning. Hebrews 10:25: gather, so we remind each other. Be there for the brother who needs to fast and the sister who needs to feast.

Q7 — New, unshrunk cloth tears the old garment; fermenting new wine bursts old, inflexible wineskins. The new way of repentance, centred on Jesus, can’t be patched onto the old. Romans 13:14: put on the Lord Jesus Christ. Levi did not take a few days’ leave to organise the feast; he left his job. “All or nothing.”

Q8 — If Jesus is Lord, he decides how much of life is his: all of it. Don’t add Jesus to an unchanged life. Ask: has there been any change, or am I still living the old way? Saying sorry is only the beginning of a new life; the heart of repentance is not a Jesus patch, but the Person of Jesus.
