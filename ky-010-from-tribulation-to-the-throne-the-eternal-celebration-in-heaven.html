From Tribulation to the Throne: The Eternal Celebration in Heaven

Revelation 7:9–17 · Sunday 30 August 2026 — St Paul’s Anglican Church Kogarah

Big Idea: Whatever you are going through right now is real, but it is not the end of your story. Jesus the Lamb has already washed you clean with his own blood, and he is taking you to a celebration in heaven where God himself wipes the tears off your face.

Aim: By the end of tonight students will…

1. See that the crowd in heaven comes from every nation, tribe, people and language — so cliques, casual racism and “people like us” have no future.

2. Understand that the white robe is a gift washed in Jesus’ blood, not a reward for being good enough.

3. Name one hard thing you are actually carrying, and hold it next to the promise that God will wipe away every tear.

You'll need: Bibles (CSB), pens, a whiteboard or big sheet of paper, about 60–75 minutes.

Connect (10 min)

  • What is the biggest crowd you have ever been in — a concert, a stadium, Vivid, a packed train at Kogarah station? What did it actually feel like to be one face among that many people?
  • When something goes badly wrong at school, who is the first person you tell? And be honest: do you want advice from them, or do you just want them to sit there with you?

Read

9 After this I looked, and there was a vast multitude from every nation, tribe, people, and language, which no one could number, standing before the throne and before the Lamb. They were robed in white with palm branches in their hands.

10 And they cried out in a loud voice: Salvation belongs to our God, who is seated on the throne, and to the Lamb!

11 All the angels stood around the throne, and along with the elders and the four living creatures they fell facedown before the throne and worshiped God,

12 saying, Amen! Blessing and glory and wisdom and thanksgiving and honor and power and strength be to our God forever and ever. Amen.

13 Then one of the elders asked me, “Who are these people robed in white, and where did they come from?”

14 I said to him, “Sir, you know.” Then he told me: These are the ones coming out of the great tribulation. They washed their robes and made them white in the blood of the Lamb.

15 For this reason they are before the throne of God, and they serve him day and night in his temple. The one seated on the throne will shelter them:

16 They will no longer hunger; they will no longer thirst; the sun will no longer strike them, nor will any scorching heat.

17 For the Lamb who is at the center of the throne will shepherd them; he will guide them to springs of the waters of life, and God will wipe away every tear from their eyes.

Revelation 7:9–17, Christian Standard Bible

Context

John is an old man locked up on a prison island called Patmos, because he would not stop talking about Jesus. Back on the mainland his friends are being killed by the Roman Empire and the church is frightened. Right in the middle of that, God opens a door in heaven and shows him how the story ends. Revelation is not a code to crack — it is a picture painted to keep scared Christians going.

Discuss

  1. v.9 — Who is in this crowd, and how many of them are there? What four words does John use to say where they come from?
  2. v.9 — They are standing, not hiding. What are they wearing, and what are they holding? What do you reckon those two things say about them?
  3. v.10 — They shout that salvation belongs to God. Why does it matter that they don’t shout “we made it”?
  4. vv.11–12 — Angels are the highest creatures in the universe, and they put their faces on the ground. What does that say about worship — and about us?
  5. v.14 — The elder says these people came out of the great tribulation. What does that little word “out” change about how you think about hard times?
  6. v.14 — Blood stains things red — it doesn’t bleach them. So what is “washed their robes and made them white in the blood of the Lamb” actually saying about how you get clean?
  7. vv.15–16 — List everything God does for these people in these two verses. Which one hits you hardest right now, and why?
  8. v.17 — Notice it says God will wipe away every tear — not that there were never any tears. Why does that difference matter when a friend of yours is grieving?

Pray

  • Pray by name for your three friends who aren’t Christians yet — that God’s kindness would lead them to repentance, and that you’d get one chance to point them to Jesus.

Leader's Notes

Some of your students are carrying more than you know — a parent’s illness, a family falling apart, a friend who self-harms, exam terror. This passage does not tell them to cheer up. It tells them their tears have been counted and will one day be wiped away by God himself. Say that warmly and let it sit.

Some in the room may not be Christians yet. Do not corner anyone. Point joyfully to Jesus: the reason anyone in that crowd is standing there is the Lamb, not their record. That is good news for a room full of teenagers who are exhausted from performing.

Two likely wrong turns

  • Turning “the great tribulation” into a timeline puzzle. Someone will want to argue about end-times charts, and it will eat your whole night. Acknowledge Christians read it in several ways (first-century persecution, the distress before Christ returns, or the whole of a believer’s life — Acts 14:22), then steer hard back to the word that matters here: they came OUT of it.
  • Hearing “God will wipe away every tear” as “so stop crying”. That is the opposite of what the verse says. The tears are real and God does not delete them; he comes and wipes them away himself. If a student is grieving, this verse is a promise, not a rebuke.

Question helps

Q1. A crowd no one could number, from every nation, tribe, people and language. Contrast it with the 144,000 of verses 1—8 — a complete but limited number picturing Old Testament Israel. Now the picture bursts its banks: believers of every age, place and race.

Q2. White robes and palm branches. White in Revelation means purity, victory and resurrection glory — and they are standing, which in the Old Testament a sinner could never do before a holy God. Palm branches mean victory and joy (Leviticus 23:40; John 12:13), the imagery of the Feast of Tabernacles.

Q3. Because “we made it” would make salvation their achievement. Salvation belongs to God — he is its source and its owner. The crowd is saying God has won, the Lamb has won, and we got included in his victory.

Q4. Worship is about making God great, not making ourselves great. If the highest creatures in the universe are facedown, our anxiety about how well we performed on Sunday is a category error. Sunday is a worship already in progress in heaven that we join.

Q5. “Out” means they have been through it and already overcome. Tribulation is not the end point; the throne is. That is the “already and not yet” — they have come out, we are still in, but the vision guarantees we will come out too.

Q6. It is deliberately impossible. The verb has the sense of being bleached, being made white — something done to you. Christ’s death becomes your acquittal and his blood accomplishes your cleanness (1 John 1:7). You did not weave the robe and you did not scrub it.

Q7. Shelters them, no more hunger, no more thirst, no scorching sun (fulfilling Isaiah 49:10), serving him in his presence without ever burning out. Let them answer honestly about which one lands — don’t correct their choice.

Q8. Because it takes their grief seriously. Every tear was really there. God does not pretend it away; he stoops down and wipes it. So the right thing to say to a grieving friend is not “don’t cry” but “God has counted every one of those.”
