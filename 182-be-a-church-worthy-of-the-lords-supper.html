Did you know in some cultures, some food have special meanings?

For example, many Chinese people like noodles.

Why do we like noodles?

Not only do noodles taste nice but long noodles symbolise longevity.

The longer the noodle you have, the longer your life will be, or so it’s said.

(Just to be clear – that’s not from the Bible!)

How about baklava?

Living here in Kogarah, I’ve often seen baklava sold in Greek shops.

Baklava is a Greek desert.

I found out that traditionally baklava is made up of 33 layers.

Each year representing a year of the life of Jesus.

How about the Lord’s Supper?

What’s the meaning behind the Lord’s Supper?

Often, we have people, often young people, even young children, asking whether they can have it or not.

I remember one time when a child got upset because they didn’t get any!

They’re probably thinking, “That’s not fair! Why do the adults get some and we don’t!

“What’s the big deal about that little piece of bread and that small cup of wine”.

Well, if it’s wine, then the children definitely shouldn’t drink it – it’s no good for them.

Depending on how it’s given, if they take a large gulp from the Common Cup, it might even be dangerous!

In fact, in today’s passage, the Bible tells us that the Lord’s Supper is dangerous to some people.

Not little children, but grown-ups as well.

Indeed, not just any grown-ups, but important grown-ups who come to Church.

How can the bread and the wine be dangerous to anyone, let alone adult Christians?

Division at the supper

Last week, Paul praised the Corinthians for holding on to the traditions he delivered to them.

But this week, he could not praise them.

In fact, he tells them that their gatherings are so terrible that when they come together, it’s not for the better for the worse.

Look with me at verse 17-18,

“Now in giving this instruction I do not praise you, since you come together not for the better but for the worse. 18 For to begin with, I hear that when you come together as a church there are divisions among you, and in part I believe it.”

When the Corinthians gather, they gather not as one Church but in divided groups.

It wasn’t that long ago in America when white people wouldn’t eat with people of colour.

In fact, many restaurants are segregated:

there was a door on the left for the white folk

and then there was a door on the right, probably a smaller one, for people of colour.

While that changed with the laws in the 1960’s, we still see remnants of the racial segregation in America today.

Sadly, we see this in many Churches still.

As recent as 2001, as many as 87% of Christian Churches in the United States were completely made up of only white or African-American Parishioners.

The trend is slowly changing to have more integration in the Church, but as the civil right leader Martin Luther King had observed back in 1968, "Eleven o'clock on Sunday morning is the most segregated hour of America."

Today, in many American Churches, and not a few Australian Churches too, Sunday morning might still be the most segregated hour.

While the Church should be an example of love and acceptance, we have often failed to love people who aren’t like us.

We see this even in the earliest days of the Church.

That’s what Paul’s talking referring to in 1 Corinthians.

But rather than colour, there was something else segregating the Church.

It was their disregard for those who come late to the Lord’s supper.

Look with me at verse 19, “Indeed, it is necessary that there be factions among you, so that those who are approved may be recognized among you.”

This was no small matter for Paul.

The way they’re disregarding those who come late shows that there are two groups among them; those approved by God and those who aren’t approved by God.

Paul has strong words for those who aren’t approved by God.

Their behaviour at the Lord’s Supper is so appalling that what they’re having cannot be properly called the Lord’s Supper.

What’s the terrible behaviour?

They don’t wait for others at the Lord’s Supper.

Verse 21, “For at the meal, each one eats his own supper. So one person is hungry while another gets drunk!”

When they gather for the Lord’s Supper, they didn’t just have pieces of bread and little cups of wine.

It was more like a meal.

It was supposed to be a symbol of unity and love.

But instead, they didn’t wait for one another.

Most likely what happened was those who were wealthy enough didn’t have to work and so they had plenty of time to make it to the Lord’s Supper.

But the poor in the society, those who had to work as slaves, had to finish work before they can come to Church.

Those who were poor probably couldn’t get there before the rich and so by the time they get to the Church it would’ve been too late.

Those who were rich and part of the elite would start their meal before the poor got there, had their fill, get drunk even.

By the time the poor and the slaves came, they got nothing.

They had to remain being hungry.

Though the rich and the elite say that their servants of Christ and that they’re thankful for what Jesus has done when they take the Lord’s Supper,

Their action shows otherwise.

The contents of the Lord’s Supper, the bread and the wine, are important but the way you have it, the manner by which you receive it, is also very important.

For the contents of the meal and the manner by which we take the meal go together.

Just as when you give a message, it’s not just what you say but how you say it.

In the Lord’s supper, it’s not just about what you do but how you do it.

While the way you behave at the Lord’s Supper itself is important

your actions leading to it as well as your actions after it are also very important.

They will show whether you have truly understood the significance of the Lord’s Supper or not.

And the point that Paul focuses on again and again here is your relation to the people in the Church.

The Lord’s Supper is not about your individual act of worship before God.

Rather the Lord’s Supper is a very communal act, it’s the Holy Communion.

It’s supposed to be a demonstration of the unity of God’s people as we join in thanks for what Jesus has done for us.

When we divide ourselves, segregate ourselves according to our social status, according to our wealth, or according to any barriers of our own making,

we make a mockery of the Lord’s Supper.

We come together not for better but worse when one group at Church are more privileged than another.

When we divide ourselves according to man’s standards, humiliating a group less privileged than us,

we mock Jesus even as we call him Lord in the Lord’s Supper.

Here’s the first question for this week:

Wealth can segregate the Church. Social standing can segregate the Church.

What other unhelpful dividing lines can segregate the Church?

Talk with the person next to you and message your answer to me via Zoom, Whatsapp or Wechat.

What other unhelpful dividing lines can segregate the Church?

Yes, these can all cause segregations in the Church.

I’ve seen churches were the wealthy call the shots.

I’ve seen church members who look down on others who have different tastes in music.

I’ve seen church members who dismiss others who don’t haven’t achieved the same education level as they.

I’ve even seen church members who dismiss others who have achieved a higher education, usually out of jealousy.

And the list can go on.

There are many different ways we can look down upon and humiliate others in the Church.

But know this: Humiliation has no place in the Church.

It does not please God; rather it infuriates God to see people who call themselves Christians humiliate Christians.

It infuriates God especially in light of all that He’s done for us in the death of Jesus,

which is the very thing that we’re proclaiming at the Lord’s Supper

Proclaiming the Death of the Lord at the Supper

Look with me at verses 23-24,

“For I received from the Lord what I also passed on to you: On the night when he was betrayed, the Lord Jesus took bread, 24 and when he had given thanks, broke it, and said, “This is my body, which is for you. Do this in remembrance of me.”

That’s what we do at the Lord’s Supper, we remember what happened to Jesus on the night he was betrayed.

We remember that Jesus gave his body for us.

The Lord’s Supper is like a dramatized message.

What’s a dramatized message?

It’s a message that is done through words and actions.

It’s like the Haka that the New Zealand Rugby Union team does every time they play an international game.

You know the one, the ka mate.

Does anyone know it? Can anyone perform it? Here’s a short clip of it.

It’s a very powerful sounding song, isn’t it?

It was written by a war leader to celebrate his escape from death.

Now it’s sung by the All Blacks before their games to show their opponents they are afraid of nothing,

that they’re ready for a battle.

That’s what I call a dramatised message.

The Lord’s Supper, too, is a dramatised message and it’s an even more powerful message than the Ka mate.

Like the Ka mate, it talks about escape from death.

But unlike the Ka mate, it talks about how the very Son of God saved us from death.

Jesus gave up his body for us to save us from death.

Jesus is our Escape from death.

Look at the way the Bible describes his rescue: It begins with the words “ on the night he was betrayed.”

From the first time the Lord had this Supper with the disciples, it was fraught with danger.

One of his closest friends, one of his own disciples, who was sitting at the very same table at the Lord’s Supper,

Was going to betray him that night.

Another disciple was going to disown him; the rest were going to leave him in the next few hours.

These were not the noblest, the wisest and certainly not the wealthiest group of people.

From a human point of view, they do not deserve a seat on the Lord’s Table.

And yet, the Lord Jesus welcomes them to His Supper.

In a few hours, he would die for them to save them, so that they’ll have eternal life.

And He wants them to have this supper to remember Him.

Every time they have the Lord’s Supper, he wants them to remember that He has come to save them and everyone one who believes in Jesus.

Every time they have the Lord’s Supper he wants them to remember that he’s there with them, and that he’s never going to leave them.

He wants them to remember that this is a meal, which symbolises the restored relationship, between God and men,

Is paid by his body and blood.

It’s his meal.

It’s a meal that celebrates that a new beginning in the relationship between God and men had begun.

Look with me at verses 25-26, “ In the same way also he took the cup, after supper, and said, “This cup is the new covenant in my blood. Do this, as often as you drink it, in remembrance of me.” 26 For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord’s death until he comes.”

This meal celebrates a new covenant, a new promise that God’s made with mankind in Jesus.

It’s a new covenant that promises forgiveness, a covenant that the God’s given in the Old Testament through his prophet Jeremiah when he said,

"For I will forgive their wickedness and will remember their sins no more."

It’s a promise for those who believe and belong to Jesus.

It’s not just a religious ritual for people to do at church and it’s certainly not for unbelievers.

It’s a promise that Christians celebrate and proclaim every time we have the Lord’s Supper.

It’s our dramatised message.

In the Lord’s Supper, we proclaim that Jesus has died for us and that he’s our escape from death.

It feels like it’s been a very long time since we’ve had the Lord’s Supper here at Church.

It was so good to see our English brothers and sisters as well as our Chinese brothers and sisters, the young and the old, all coming together as one in Christ.

That’s our message: We’ve been saved by the death of Jesus, and we proclaim his death until he comes.

More than that, as we come to the Lord’s Supper, we’ll be a people shaped by the death of Jesus.

Like our Lord Jesus, we long to share the Lord’s Supper with our brothers and sisters,

Regardless of their wealth, regardless of their shortcomings,

Just as Jesus saved us despite of our wealth and our shortcomings.

We come to the Lord’s Supper with a heart shaped by the death of Jesus.

And even though we can’t come physically to celebrate the Lord’s Supper at the moment,

Many of us are still serving our Lord with the same attitude.

Many of our brothers and sisters serve by sending in videos of their prayers, their Bible readings and their music.

For those who’ve done it, you know that it takes a lot more time to prepare them than simply turning up on Sundays.

We thank you for serving Jesus and serving us in this way.

A special thanks to the musicians who not only practice but have to record their parts a number of times to get it right.

We thank you for your service to Christ and to us.

A special thanks to our Translators who spend hours to translate the so that we can all hear and understand the sermon We thank you for your service to Christ and to us.

That’s what it looks like to live out the meaning of the Lord’s Supper.

Jesus gave up his life sacrificially for his people; As his people, we’ll follow in his footsteps and do the same.

That’s the community we want to be here at St Paul’s.

We want to be a community that reflects Christ’s death, not only at the Lord’s Supper, not only when we gather physically together,

But also as we gather for online Church,

As we gather for online Growth groups,

Our online KOG Junior and KOG Youth,

Our online Prayer meetings.

We want to be a community that proclaims Christ’s death by living in light of Christ’s death.

To do that, we must continue to examine our lives, for if we don’t, we will come under judgement.

Judgement for the unworthy at the Supper

The Bible tells us that there will be judgement for those who take the Supper in an unworthy manner.

Look with me at verse 27-28,

“So, then, whoever eats the bread or drinks the cup of the Lord in an unworthy manner will be guilty of sin against the bodya and blood of the Lord. 28 Let a person examine himself; in this way let him eat the bread and drink from the cup.”

If we receive the Lord’s Supper in an unworthy manner, we will be guilty of sinning against the body and the blood of the Lord.

How do we receive the Lord’s Supper in an unworthy manner?

In the context of this passage, we receive the Lord’s Supper in an unworthy manner when we look down on others at Church.

We mock Jesus when we humiliate those who are his.

When we discriminate those who are different from us and segregate among ourselves, we risk sinning against the body and the blood of the Lord.

It’s like this: can you imagine the All-Blacks performing the Ka mate with their heads bowed?

Can you imagine them reciting the ka mate, with anything less than the fiercest intensity; can you imagine them laughing while they’re doing the ka mate?

If they do these things, they will be booed, they will take them off the team, their own people, the New Zealanders, will heap abuse on them.

Why? Because they gave the ka mate in an unworthy manner.

That’s what happens if Christians are more concerned about themselves than the good of other Christians.

That’s what happens if Christians come to the Lord’s Supper as a divided people.

Even though their lips may be saying the right thing, their actions will speak otherwise.

Though they may want people to hear their words, the people will see their actions first, and actions speak much louder than words.

Though they may taking the bread and drinking the wine, they’re not remembering the Lord’s death.

What will people hear when people come to the Lord’s Supper in an unworthy manner?

They will hear that the church doesn’t really believe in the gospel.

Even though they’re saying the words, and taking the Lord’s supper, a Church that discriminates among themselves is not proclaiming the Lord’s Death.

They’re not receiving the Lord’s Supper in the way it was meant to be.

They’re receiving the Lord’s Supper in an unworthy manner.

And they will be judged by God himself.

Look with me at Verses 29-30: For whoever eats and drinks without recognizing the body, eats and drinks judgment on himself. 30 This is why many are sick and ill among you, and many have fallen asleep.

God will judge his people if they receive the Lord’s Supper in an unworthy manner.

God has punished his people in the past with sickness and even death.

God can still judge his people today.

While not every sickness is a direct result of our disobedience, even so, whenever we do have sickness, we need to turn back to God and seek his mercy.

No sickness comes to us apart from God’s loving purpose; each one is sent by Him according to his loving purpose.

I remember an older Christian man who was immensely talented and greatly blessed by God.

He was a wonderful Christian musician and taught many students how to produce wonderful Christian music.

However, one day, he was diagnosed with cancer and he couldn’t speak, let alone sing, the very thing he was blessed to do.

In his frustrations, a Christian sister had told him that perhaps this was God’s lesson to teach him humility.

It was a very hard lesson but he learnt it and shared his story with many of us afterwards.

And we notice that there was a changed man; still a very talent man but also a very sensitive and humble man.

God is serious about us living our lives in light of the death of Christ.

He’s serious about what we proclaim as we come together as Church, not only as we receive the Lord’s Supper,

but more broadly as we share our lives together, whether it’s online for now or in person later.

If we live come before him in an unworthy manner, he will judge us.

And so, listen to the warning in verses 31-32, “If we were properly judging ourselves, we would not be judged, 32 but when we are judged by the Lord, we are disciplined, so that we may not be condemned with the world.

Examine yourself, judge yourself so that you won’t be judged.

And even when we are judged, we can rest assure that we’re judged so that we won’t be condemned with the world.

Practically for the Corinthians, it would mean waiting for one another at the Lord’s Supper.

For us, it will also mean waiting patiently when we do have the Lord’s Supper.

But more broadly, it will mean recognising other people’s importance to God.

The person sitting next to you at Church, the person next to you on Zoom, is not beneath you.

He’s a child of God for whom Christ died.

Love the person, respect the person, so that you won’t come under judgement.

And so, friends, come to the Lord’s Supper, not with a mindset of segregation, not looking down on others, not discriminating against another person because of their wealth or their social status or their culture or their background,

But as one people, come together to proclaim the death of the Lord.

What does this mean for us?

Live your life in a manner worthy of the Lord’s Supper.

Live your life in a manner worthy of the Lord’s Supper.

Over the past few weeks, my family often talk about where we would go for dinner once lockdown is over.

What food will we get first? Who will we eat with?

We’re looking forward to that day when we can sit down for a meal with many of our family members, whom we haven’t seen for a long time.

But even more than a meal at a restaurant, I’m looking forward to having the Lord’s Supper with many of you.

I look forward to the day when we can come back together in person as one people at Church.

I look forward to proclaiming the Lord’s death with you all as we gather again.

But until that day, until the next Lord’s Supper, let’s live our lives out now in preparation for it.

Let’s examine our lives and our attitudes towards one another as we prepare to receive the Lord’s supper.

Don’t wait until we come back to show your love and concern for one another!

Pick up the phone, send someone from Church a message, give someone from Church a phone call.

Over the past week, I’ve messaged and called brothers in Christ who had been going through difficult times.

I wished I called them earlier so that I could encourage them earlier.

What about you? Who from Church haven’t you contacted for a while?

How can you show concern for them?

Or more importantly, how does Jesus want you to show concern for them?

Here are the three questions for this week:

Do I discriminate against other people at Church?

How am I coming to Church (online and in person) with a readiness to proclaim the Lord’s death?

How am I living a life worthy of the Lord’s Supper?

Friends, Jesus had died to invite you to share his meal.

He died to save you from death.

Live your life in a manner worthy of the Lord’s Supper.
